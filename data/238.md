["\n", "III\n", "\n", " Oriental Hotel\nYokohama. \n10th July, 1893.\n", "\nDear  Alasinga,  Balaji, G. G., Banking Corporation, and All My Madras Friends, \n", "\nExcuse my not keeping you constantly informed of my movements. One is so\nbusy  every day, and especially myself who am quite new to the life of\npossessing things and taking care of them. That consumes so much of my\nenergy. It is really an awful botheration.\n", "\nFrom Bombay we reached Colombo. Our steamer remained in port for nearly the\nwhole day, and we took the opportunity of getting off to have a look at the\ntown. We drove through the streets, and the only thing I remember was a\ntemple in which was a very gigantic Murti (image) of the Lord Buddha in a\nreclining posture, entering Nirv\u00e2na....\n", "\nThe next station was Penang, which is only a strip of land along the sea in\nthe body of the Malaya Peninsula. The Malayas are all Mohammedans and in old\ndays  were noted pirates and quite a dread to merchantmen. But now the\nleviathan guns of modern turreted battleships have forced the Malayas to\nlook about for more peaceful pursuits. On our way from Penang to Singapore,\nwe had glimpses of Sumatra with its high mountains, and the Captain pointed\nout to me several places as the favourite haunts of pirates in days gone by.\nSingapore is the capital of the Straits Settlements. It has a fine botanical\ngarden with the most splendid collection of palms. The beautiful fan-like\npalm,  called  the  traveller's palm, grows here in abundance, and the\nbread-fruit tree everywhere. The celebrated mangosteen is as plentiful here\nas mangoes in Madras, but mango is nonpareil. The people here are not half\nso  dark as the people of Madras, although so near the line. Singapore\npossesses a fine museum too.\n", "\nHong Kong next. You feel that you have reached China, the Chinese element\npredominates so much. All labour, all trade seems to be in their hands. And\nHong  Kong is real China. As soon as the steamer casts anchor, you are\nbesieged with hundreds of Chinese boats to carry you to the land. These\nboats with two helms are rather peculiar. The boatman lives in the boat with\nhis family. Almost always, the wife is at the helms, managing one with her\nhands and the other with one of her feet. And in ninety per cent of cases,\nyou find a baby tied to her back, with the hands and feet of the little Chin\nleft free. It is a quaint sight to see the little John Chinaman dangling\nvery quietly from his mother's back, whilst she is now setting with might\nand main, now pushing heavy loads, or jumping with wonderful agility from\nboat to boat. And there is such a rush of boats and steamlaunches coming in\nand going out. Baby John is every moment put into the risk of having his\nlittle head pulverised, pigtail and all; but he does not care a fig. This\nbusy life seems to have no charm for him, and he is quite content to learn\nthe anatomy of a bit of rice-cake given to him from time to time by the\nmadly busy mother. The Chinese child is quite a philosopher and calmly goes\nto work at an age when your Indian boy can hardly crawl on all fours. He has\nlearnt the philosophy of necessity too well. Their extreme poverty is one of\nthe causes why the Chinese and the Indians have remained in a state of\nmummified civilisation. To an ordinary Hindu or Chinese, everyday necessity\nis too hideous to allow him to think of anything else.\n", "\nHong Kong is a very beautiful town. It is built on the slopes of hills and\non the tops too, which are much cooler than the city. There is an almost\nperpendicular tramway going to the top of the hill, dragged by wire-rope and\nsteam-power.\n", "\nWe remained three days at Hong Kong and went to see Canton, which is eighty\nmiles up a river. The river is broad enough to allow the biggest steamers to\npass through. A number of Chinese steamers ply between Hong Kong and Canton.\nWe took passage on one of these in the evening and reached Canton early in\nthe morning. What a scene of bustle and life! What an immense number of\nboats almost covering the waters! And not only those that are carrying on\nthe trade, but hundreds of others which serve as houses to live in. And\nquite a lot of them so nice and big! In fact, they are big houses two or\nthree storeys high, with verandahs running round and streets between, and\nall floating!\n", "\nWe landed on a strip of ground given by the Chinese Government to foreigners\nto live in. Around us on both sides of the river for miles and miles is the\nbig city \u2014 a wilderness of human beings, pushing, struggling, surging,\nroaring. But with all its population, all its activity, it is the dirtiest\ntown I saw, not in the sense in which a town is called dirty in India, for\nas to that not a speck of filth is allowed by the Chinese to go waste; but\nbecause of the Chinaman, who has, it seems, taken a vow never to bathe!\nEvery house is a shop, people living only on the top floor. The streets are\nvery very narrow, so that you almost touch the shops on both sides as you\npass. At every ten paces you find meat-stalls, and there are shops which\nsell cat's and dog's meat. Of course, only the poorest classes of Chinamen\neat dog or cat.\n", "\nThe Chinese ladies can never be seen. They have got as strict a zenana as\nthe Hindus of Northern India; only the women of the labouring classes can be\nseen. Even amongst these, one sees now and then a woman with feet smaller\nthan those of your youngest child, and of course they cannot be said to\nwalk, but hobble.\n", "\nI went to see several Chinese temples. The biggest in Canton is dedicated to\nthe  memory of the first Buddhistic Emperor and the five hundred first\ndisciples of Buddhism. The central figure is of course Buddha, and next\nbeneath Him is seated the Emperor, and ranging on both sides are the statues\nof the disciples, all beautifully carved out of wood.\n", "\nFrom Canton I returned back to Hong Kong, and from thence to Japan. The\nfirst port we touched was Nagasaki. We landed for a few hours and drove\nthrough the town. What a contrast! The Japanese are one of the cleanliest\npeoples on earth. Everything is neat and tidy. Their streets are nearly all\nbroad, straight, and regularly paved. Their little houses are cage-like, and\ntheir pine-covered evergreen little hills form the background of almost\nevery town and village. The short-statured, fair-skinned, quaintly-dressed\nJaps, their movements, attitudes, gestures, everything is picturesque. Japan\nis the land of the picturesque! Almost every house has a garden at the back,\nvery  nicely laid out according to Japanese fashion with small shrubs,\ngrass-plots, small artificial waters, and small stone bridges.\n", "\nFrom Nagasaki to Kobe. Here I gave up the steamer and took the land-route to\nYokohama, with a view to see the interior of Japan.\n", "\nI have seen three big cities in the interior \u2014 Osaka, a great manufacturing\ntown, Kyoto, the former capital, and Tokyo, the present capital. Tokyo is\nnearly twice the size of Calcutta with nearly double the population.\n", "\nNo foreigner is allowed to travel in the interior without a passport.\n", "\nThe Japanese seem now to have fully awakened themselves to the necessity of\nthe present times. They have now a thoroughly organised army equipped with\nguns which one of their own officers has invented and which is said to be\nsecond to none. Then, they are continually increasing their navy. I have\nseen a tunnel nearly a mile long, bored by a Japanese engineer.\n", "\nThe match factories are simply a sight to see, and they are bent upon making\neverything they want in their own country. There is a Japanese line of\nsteamers plying between China and Japan, which shortly intends running\nbetween Bombay and Yokohama.\n", "\nI  saw quite a lot of temples. In every temple there are some Sanskrit\nMantras written in Old Bengali characters. Only a few of the priests know\nSanskrit. But they are an intelligent sect. The modern rage for progress has\npenetrated even the priesthood. I cannot write what I have in my mind about\nthe Japs in one short letter. Only I want that numbers of our young men\nshould  pay  a  visit to Japan and China every year. Especially to the\nJapanese, India is still the dreamland of everything high and good. And you,\nwhat are you? . . . talking twaddle all your lives, vain talkers, what are\nyou? Come, see these people, and then go and hide your faces in shame. A\nrace of dotards, you lose your caste if you come out! Sitting down these\nhundreds of years with an ever-increasing load of crystallised superstition\non  your  heads,  for  hundreds of years spending all your energy upon\ndiscussing the touchableness or untouchableness of this food or that, with\nall humanity crushed out of you by the continuous social tyranny of ages \u2014\nwhat are you? And what are you doing now? . . . promenading the sea-shores\nwith books in your hands \u2014 repeating undigested stray bits of European\nbrainwork, and the whole soul bent upon getting a thirty-rupee clerkship, or\nat best becoming a lawyer \u2014 the height of young India's ambition \u2014 and every\nstudent with a whole brood of hungry children cackling at his heels and\nasking for bread! Is there not water enough in the sea to drown you, books,\ngowns, university diplomas, and all?\n", "\nCome, be men! Kick out the priests who are always against progress, because\nthey would never mend, their hearts would never become big. They are the\noffspring of centuries of superstition and tyranny. Root out priest craft\nfirst. Come, be men! Come out of your narrow holes and have a look abroad.\nSee how nations are on the march! Do you love man? Do you love your country?\nThen come, let us struggle for higher and better things; look not back, no,\nnot even if you see the dearest and nearest cry. Look not back, but forward!\n", "\nIndia wants the sacrifice of at least a thousand or her young men \u2014 men,\nmind,  and not brutes. The English Government has been the instrument,\nbrought over here by the Lord, to break your crystallised civilisation, and\nMadras supplied the first men who helped in giving the English a footing.\nHow many men, unselfish, thorough-going men, is Madras ready now to supply,\nto  struggle  unto life and death to bring about a new state of things\nsympathy for the poor, and bread to their hungry mouths, enlightenment to\nthe people at large \u2014 and struggle unto death to make men of them who have\nbeen brought to the level of beasts, by the tyranny of your forefathers?\n", "\n", "Yours etc.,\n Vivekananda. \n", "\nPS.  Calm  and  silent  and  steady  work, and no newspaper humbug, no\nname-making, you must always remember.\n", "\n", "\n"]
