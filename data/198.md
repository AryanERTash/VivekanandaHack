["\n", "SKETCH OF THE LIFE OF PAVHARI BABA\n", "\nTo help the suffering world was the gigantic task to which the Buddha gave\nprominence, brushing aside for the time being almost all other phases of\nreligion; yet he had to spend years in self-searching to realise the great\ntruth of the utter hollowness of clinging to a selfish individuality. A more\nunselfish and untiring worker is beyond our most sanguine imagination: yet\nwho had harder struggles to realise the meaning of things than he? It holds\ngood in all times that the greater the work, the more must have been the\npower of realisation behind. Working out the details of an already laid out\nmasterly plan may not require much concentrated thought to back it, but the\ngreat impulses are only transformed great concentrations. The theory alone\nperhaps is sufficient for small exertions, but the push that creates the\nripple is very different from the impulsion that raises the wave, and yet\nthe ripple is only the embodiment of a bit of the power that generates the\nwave.\n", "\nFacts, naked facts, gaunt and terrible may be; truth, bare truth, though its\nvibrations may snap every chord of the heart; motive selfless and sincere,\nthough to reach it, limb after limb has to be lopped off \u2014 such are to be\narrived  at,  found, and gained, before the mind on the lower plane of\nactivity can raise huge work-waves. The fine accumulates round itself the\ngross  as  it  rolls  on through time and becomes manifest, the unseen\ncrystallises into the seen, the possible becomes the practical, the cause\nthe effect, and thought, muscular work.\n", "\nThe cause, held back by a thousand circumstances, will manifest itself,\nsooner or later, as the effect; and potent thought, however powerless at\npresent, will have its glorious day on the plane of material activity. Nor\nis  the  standard  correct  which judges of everything by its power to\ncontribute to our sense-enjoyment.\n", "\nThe lower the animal, the more is its enjoyment in the senses, the more it\nlives in the senses. Civilisation, true civilization, should mean the power\nof taking the animal-man out of his sense-life \u2014 by giving him visions and\ntastes of planes much higher \u2014 and not external comforts.\n", "\nMan knows this instinctively. He may not formulate it to himself under all\ncircumstances.  He  may form very divergent opinions about the life of\nthought.  But  it  is  there, pressing itself to the front in spite of\neverything, making him pay reverence to the hoodoo-worker, the medicine-man,\nthe magician, the priest, or the professor of science. The growth of man can\nonly be gauged by his power of living in the higher atmosphere where the\nsenses are left behind, the amount of the pure thought-oxygen his lungs can\nbreathe in, and the amount of time he can spend on that height.\n", "\nAs it is, it is an obvious fact that, with the exception of what is taken up\nby the necessities of life, the man of culture is loth to spend his time on\nso-called comforts, and even necessary actions are performed with lessened\nzeal, as the process moves forward.\n", "\nEven luxuries are arranged according to ideas and ideals, to make them\nreflect as much of thought-life as possible \u2014 and this is Art.\n", "\n\"As the one fire coming into the universe is manifesting itself in every\nform, and yet is more besides\" \u2014 yes, infinitely more besides! A bit, only a\nsmall bit, of infinite thought can be made to descend to the plane of matter\nto minister to our comfort \u2014 the rest will not allow itself to be rudely\nhandled. The superfine always eludes our view and laughs at our attempts to\nbring it down. In this case, Mohammed must go to the mountain, and no \"nay\".\nMan  must  raise himself to that higher plane if he wants to enjoy its\nbeauties, to bathe in its light, to feel his life pulsating in unison with\nthe Cause-Life of the universe.\n", "\nIt is knowledge that opens the door to regions of wonder, knowledge that\nmakes  a god of an animal: and that knowledge which brings us to That,\n\"knowing which everything else is known\" (the heart of all knowledge \u2014 whose\npulsation  brings  life  to all sciences \u2014 the science of religion) is\ncertainly the highest, as it alone can make man live a complete and perfect\nlife in thought. Blessed be the land which has styled it \"supreme science\"!\n", "\nThe principle is seldom found perfectly expressed in the practical, yet the\nideal is never lost. On the one hand, it is our duty never to lose sight of\nthe ideal, whether we can approach it with sensible steps, or crawl towards\nit with imperceptible motion: on the other hand, the truth is, it is always\nloosening in front of us \u2014 though we try our best to cover its light with\nour hands before our eyes.\n", "\nThe  life  of  the practical is in the ideal. It is the ideal that has\npenetrated the whole of our lives, whether we philosophise, or perform the\nhard,  everyday  duties  of life. The rays of the ideal, reflected and\nrefracted in various straight or tortuous lines, are pouring in through\nevery  aperture  and windhole, and consciously or unconsciously, every\nfunction has to be performed in its light, every object has to be seen\ntransformed, heightened, or deformed by it. It is the ideal that has made us\nwhat we are, and will make us what we are going to be. It is the power of\nthe ideal that has enshrouded us, and is felt in our joys or sorrows, in our\ngreat acts or mean doings, in our virtues and vices.\n", "\nIf such is the power of the ideal over the practical, the practical is no\nless  potent  in  forming  the ideal. The truth of the ideal is in the\npractical. The fruition of the ideal has been through the sensing of the\npractical.  That the ideal is there is a proof of the existence of the\npractical  somehow,  somewhere. The ideal may be vaster, yet it is the\nmultiplication of little bits of the practical. The ideal mostly is the\nsummed-up, generalized, practical units.\n", "\nThe power of the ideal is in the practical. Its work on us is in and through\nthe practical. Through the practical, the ideal is brought down to our\nsense-perception, changed into a form fit for our assimilation. Of the\npractical we make the steps to rise to the ideal. On that we build our\nhopes; it gives us courage to work.\n", "\nOne man who manifests the ideal in his life is more powerful than legions\nwhose words can paint it in the most beautiful colours and spin out the\nfinest principles.\n", "\nSystems of philosophy mean nothing to mankind, or at best only intellectual\ngymnastics, unless they are joined to religion and can get a body of men\nstruggling to bring them down to practical life with more or less success.\nEven systems having not one positive hope, when taken up by groups and made\nsomewhat practical, had always a multitude; and the most elaborate positive\nsystems of thought withered away without it.\n", "\nMost of us cannot keep our activities on a par with our thought-lives. Some\nblessed ones can. Most of us seem to lose the power of work as we think\ndeeper, and the power of deep thought if we work more. That is why most\ngreat thinkers have to leave to time the practical realisation of their\ngreat ideals. Their thoughts must wait for more active brains to work them\nout and spread them. Yet, as we write, comes before us a vision of him, the\ncharioteer of Arjuna, standing in his chariot between the contending hosts,\nhis  left  hand  curbing the fiery steeds \u2014 a mail-clad warrior, whose\neagle-glance sweeps over the vast army, and as if by instinct weighs every\ndetail of the battle array of both parties \u2014 at the same time that we hear,\nas it were, falling from his lips and thrilling the awestruck Arjuna, that\nmost marvellous secret of work: \"He who finds rest in the midst of activity,\nand activity in rest, he is the wise amidst men, he the Yogi, he is the doer\nof all work\" (Gita, IV. 18).\n", "\nThis is the ideal complete. But few ever reach it. We must take things as\nthey are, therefore, and be contented to piece together different aspects of\nhuman perfection, developed in different individuals.\n", "\nIn  religion  we have the man of intense thought, of great activity in\nbringing help to others, the man of boldness and daring self-realisation,\nand the man of meekness and humility.\n", "\nThe subject of this sketch was a man of wonderful humility and intense\nself-realisation.\n", "\nBorn of Br\u00e2hmin parents in a village near Guzi, Varanasi, Pavh\u00e2ri B\u00e2b\u00e2, as\nhe  was called in after life, came to study and live with his uncle in\nGhazipur, when a mere boy. At present, Hindu ascetics are split up into the\nmain divisions of Sanny\u00e2sins, Yogis, Vair\u00e2gis, and Panthis. The Sannyasins\nare the followers of Advaitism after Shankar\u00e2ch\u00e2rya; the Yogis, though\nfollowing the Advaita system, are specialists in practicing the different\nsystems of Yoga; the Vairagis are the dualistic disciples of R\u00e2m\u00e2nuj\u00e2ch\u00e2rya\nand others; the Panthis, professing either philosophy, are orders founded\nduring  the Mohammedan rule. The uncle of Pavhari Baba belonged to the\nRamanuja or Shri sect, and was a Naishthika Brahmach\u00e2rin, i.e. one who takes\nthe vow of lifelong celibacy. He had a piece of land on the banks of the\nGanga, about two miles to the north of Ghazipur, and had established himself\nthere.  Having several nephews, he took Pavhari Baba into his home and\nadopted him, intending him to succeed to his property and position.\n", "\nNot much is known of the life of Pavhari Baba at this period. Neither does\nthere seem to have been any indication of those peculiarities which made him\nso well known in after years. He is remembered merely as a diligent student\nof Vy\u00e2karana and Ny\u00e2ya, and the theology of his sect, and as an active\nlively boy whose jollity at times found vent in hard practical jokes at the\nexpense of his fellow-students.\n", "\nThus the future saint passed his young days, going through the routine\nduties of Indian students of the old school; and except that he showed more\nthan ordinary application to his studies, and a remarkable aptitude for\nlearning languages, there was scarcely anything in that open, cheerful,\nplayful student life to foreshadow the tremendous seriousness which was to\nculminate in a most curious and awful sacrifice.\n", "\nThen something happened which made the young scholar feel, perhaps for the\nfirst time, the serious import of life, and made him raise his eyes, so long\nriveted  on books, to scan his mental horizon critically and crave for\nsomething in religion which was a fact, and not mere book-lore. His uncle\npassed  away.  One  face on which all the love of that young heart was\nconcentrated had gone, and the ardent boy, struck to the core with grief,\ndetermined to supply the gap with a vision that can never change.\n", "\nIn India, for everything, we want a Guru. Books, we Hindus are persuaded,\nare only outlines. The living secrets must be handed down from Guru to\ndisciple, in every art, in every science, much more so in religion. From\ntime immemorial earnest souls in India have always retired to secluded\nspots, to carry on uninterrupted their study of the mysteries of the inner\nlife, and even today there is scarcely a forest, a hill, or a sacred spot\nwhich rumour does not consecrate as the abode of a great sage. The saying is\nwell known:\n", "\"The water is pure that flows.\nThe monk is pure that goes.\"\n", "As a rule, those who take to the celibate religious life in India spend a\ngood deal of their life in journeying through various countries of the\nIndian continent, visiting different shrines \u2014 thus keeping themselves from\nrust, as it were, and at the same time bringing religion to the door of\neveryone. A visit to the four great sacred places, situated in the four\ncorners of India, is considered almost necessary to all who renounce the\nworld.\n", "\nAll these considerations may have had weight with our young Brahmacharin,\nbut we are sure that the chief among them was the thirst for knowledge. Of\nhis travels we know but little, except that, from his knowledge of Dravidian\nlanguages, in which a good deal of the literature of his sect is written,\nand his thorough acquaintance with the old Bengali of the Vaishnavas of Shri\nChaitanya's order, we infer that his stay in Southern India and Bengal could\nnot have been very short.\n", "\nBut on his visit to one place, the friends of his youth lay great stress. It\nwas on the top of mount Girn\u00e2r in Kathiawar, they say, that he was first\ninitiated into the mysteries of practical Yoga.\n", "\nIt was this mountain which was so holy to the Buddhists. At its foot is the\nhuge rock on which is inscribed the first-deciphered edict of the \"divinest\nof monarchs\", Asoka. Beneath it, through centuries of oblivion, lay the\nconclave of gigantic Stupas, forest covered, and long taken for hillocks of\nthe Girnar range. No less sacred is it still held by the sect of which\nBuddhism is now thought to be a revised edition, and which strangely enough\ndid  not  venture  into  the  field of architectural triumphs till its\nworld-conquering descendant had melted away into modern Hinduism. Girnar is\ncelebrated amongst Hindus as having been sanctified by the stay of the great\nAvadhuta Guru Datt\u00e2treya, and rumour has it that great and perfected Yogis\nare still to be met with by the fortunate on its top.\n", "\nThe next turning-point in the career of our youthful Brahmacharin we trace\nto the banks of the Ganga some where near  Varanasi, as the disciple of a\nSannyasin who practiced Yoga and lived in a hole dug in the high bank of the\nriver. To this yogi can be traced the after-practice of our saint, of living\ninside a deep tunnel, dug out of the ground on the bank of the Ganga near\nGhazipur. Yogis have always inculcated the advisability of living in caves\nor  other spots where the temperature is even, and where sounds do not\ndisturb the mind. We also learn that he was about the same time studying the\nAdvaita system under a Sannyasin in Varanasi.\n", "\nAfter years of travel, study, and discipline, the young Brahmacharin came\nback to the place where he had been brought up. Perhaps his uncle, if alive,\nwould have found in the face of the boy the same light which of yore a\ngreater sage saw in that of his disciple and exclaimed, \"Child, thy face\ntoday shines with the glory of Brahman!\" But those that welcomed him to his\nhome were only the companions of his boyhood \u2014 most of them gone into, and\nclaimed for ever by, the world of small thought and eternal toil.\n", "\nYet there was a change, a mysterious \u2014 to them an awe-inspiring \u2014 change, in\nthe whole character and demeanour of that school-day friend and playmate\nwhom  they  had been wont to understand. But it did not arouse in them\nemulation, or the same research. It was the mystery of a man who had gone\nbeyond this world of trouble and materialism, and this was enough. They\ninstinctively respected it and asked no questions.\n", "\nMeanwhile,  the peculiarities of the saint began to grow more and more\npronounced. He had a cave dug in the ground, like his friend near Varanasi,\nand began to go into it and remain there for hours. Then began a process of\nthe most awful dietary discipline. The whole day he worked in his little\n\u00c2shrama, conducted the worship of his beloved R\u00e2machandra, cooked good\ndinners \u2014 in which art he is said to have been extraordinarily proficient\n\u2014 distributed the whole of the offered food amongst his friends and the\npoor, looked after their comforts till night came, and when they were in\ntheir beds, the young man stole out, crossed the Ganga by swimming, and\nreached the other shore. Then he gave up going nightly to the woods on the other bank\nof the river and took more and more to his cave. For days and months, we are\ntold, he would be in the hole, absorbed in meditation, and then come out.\nNobody knows what he subsisted on during these long intervals, so the people\ncalled him Pav-\u00e2h\u00e2ri (or air-eater) B\u00e2b\u00e2 (or father).\n", "\nHe would never during his life leave this place. His fame\nbegan to spread, and to Rai Gagan Chandra Bahadur of the Opium Department,\nGhazipur \u2014 a gentleman whose innate nobility and spirituality have endeared\nhim to all \u2014 we owe our introduction to the saint.\n", "\nLike  many others in India, there was no striking or stirring external\nactivity in this life. Persons of this type\nare entirely averse to preaching what they know, for they are for ever\nconvinced that it is internal discipline alone that leads to truth, and not\nwords. The noseless saint led him to a very\nretired spot in the forest, took the razor in his hand, opened it, and with\none stroke cut off his nose, repeating in a solemn voice, 'Young man, this\nhas  been my initiation into the order.
