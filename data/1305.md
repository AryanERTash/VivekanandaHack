["\n", "CHAPTER V\n", "ON THE WAY TO BARAMULLA\n", "PERSONS: The Swami Vivekananda, Gurubhais, and a party of Europeans and\ndisciples, amongst whom were Dhira Mata, the \"Steady Mother\"; one whose name\nwas Jaya; and Nivedita.\n", "PLACE: From Bareilly to Baramulla, Kashmir.\n", "TIME: June 14 to 20, 1898.\n", "JUNE 14.\n", "We entered the Punjab next day, and great was the Swami's excitement at the\nfact. It almost seemed as if he had been born there, so close and special\nwas his love for this province. He talked of the girls at their spinning\nwheels listening to the \"So'ham! So'ham!\" \u2014 I am He! I am He! Then, by a\nswift transition he turned to the far past and unrolled for us the great\nhistoric panorama of the advance of the Greeks on the Indus, the rise of\nChandragupta and the development of the Buddhistic empire. He was determined\nthis summer to find his way to Attock and see with his own eyes the spot at\nwhich Alexander was turned back. He described to us the Gandhara sculptures,\nwhich he must have seen in the Lahore Museum the year before, and lost\nhimself in indignant repudiation of the absurd European claim that India had\never sat at the feet of Greece in things artistic.\n", "Then there were flying glimpses of long-expected cities \u2014 Ludhiana, where\ncertain trusty English disciples had lived as children; Lahore, where his\nIndian lectures had ended; and so on. We came too upon the dry gravel beds\nof many rivers and learnt that the space between one pair was called the\nDoab and the area containing them all, the Punjab.\n", "It was at twilight, crossing one of these stony tracts, that he told us of\nthat great vision which came to him years ago, while he was still new to the\nways of the life of a monk, giving back to him, as he always afterwards\nbelieved, the ancient mode of Sanskrit chanting.\n", "\"It was evening\", he said, \"in that age when the Aryans had only reached the\nIndus. I saw an old man seated on the bank of the great river. Wave upon\nwave of darkness was rolling in upon him, and he was chanting from the\nRig-Veda. Then I awoke and went on chanting. They were the tones that we\nused long ago\".\n", ". . . Those who were constantly preoccupied with imagination regarding their\nown past always aroused his contempt. But on this occasion of telling the\nstory, he gave a glimpse of it from a very different point of view.\n", "\"Shankar\u00e2ch\u00e2rya\", he was saying, \"had caught the rhythm of the Vedas, the\nnational cadence. Indeed I always imagine\", he went on suddenly with dreamy\nvoice and far-away look, \"I always imagine that he had some vision such as\nmine when he was young, and recovered the ancient music that way. Anyway,\nhis whole life's work is nothing but that, the throbbing of the beauty of\nthe Vedas and Upanishads\". . . .\n", "From Rawalpindi to Murree we went by tonga, and there we spent some days\nbefore setting out for Kashmir. Here the Swami came to the conclusion that\nany effort which he might make to induce the orthodox to accept a European\nas a fellow-disciple, or in the direction of woman's education, had better\nbe made in Bengal. The distrust of the foreigner was too strong in Punjab to\nadmit of work succeeding there. He was much occupied by this question from\ntime to time, and would sometimes remark on the paradox presented by the\nBengali combination of political antagonism to the English, and readiness to\nlove and trust. . . .\n", "JUNE 18.\n", ". . . . . .\n", "Most of the afternoon we were compelled by a storm to spend indoors;, and a\nnew chapter was opened at Dulai in our knowledge of Hinduism, for the Swami\ntold  us gravely and frankly of its modern abuses and spoke of his own\nuncompromising hostility to those evil practices which pass under the name\nof V\u00e2m\u00e2ch\u00e2ra.\n", "When we asked how Shri Ramakrishna \u2014 who never could bear to condemn the\nhope of any man \u2014 had looked at these things, he told us that \"the old man\"\nhad said, \"Well, well! But every house may have a scavengers' entrance!\" And\nhe pointed out that all sects of diabolism in any country belonged to this\nclass. . . .\n", "JUNE 19.\n", "We took it in turns to drive with the Swami in his tonga, and this next day\nseemed full of reminiscence.\n", "He talked of Brahmavidy\u00e2, the vision of the One, the Alone \u2014 Real, and told\nhow love was the only cure for evil. He had had a schoolfellow who grew up\nand became rich, but lost his health. It was an obscure disease, sapping his\nenergy and vitality daily, yet altogether baffling the skill of the doctors.\nAt last, because he knew that the Swami had always been religious, and men\nturn to religion when all else fails, he sent to beg him to come to him.\nWhen the Master reached him a curious thing happened. There came to his mind\na text: \"Him the Brahmin conquers who thinks that he is separate from the\nBrahmin. Him the Kshatriya conquers who thinks that he is separate from the\nKshatriya. And him the universe conquers who thinks that he is separate from\nthe universe\". And the sick man grasped this and recovered. \"And so\", said\nthe Swami, \"though I often say strange things and angry things, yet remember\nthat in my heart I never seriously mean to preach anything but love! All\nthese things will come right only when we realize that we love each other\".\n", "Was it then, or the day before, that talking of the great God, he told us\nhow when he was a child his mother would sigh over his naughtiness and say,\n\"So many prayers and austerities, and instead of a good soul, Shiva has sent\nme you!\" till he was hypnotized into a belief that he was really one of\nShiva's demons. He thought that for a punishment he had been banished for a\nwhile from Shiva's heaven, and that his one effort in life must be to go\nback there.\n", "His first act of sacrilege, he told us once, had been committed at the age\nof five when he embarked on a stormy argument with his mother, to the effect\nthat when his right hand was soiled with eating, it would be cleaner to lift\nhis tumbler of water with the left. For this or similar perversities her\nmost drastic remedy was to put him under the water tap and, while cold water\nwas pouring over his head, to say \"Shiva! Shiva!\" This, he said, never\nfailed of its effect. The prayer would remind him of his exile, and he would\nsay to himself, \"No, no, not this time again!\" and so return to quiet and\nobedience.\n", "He had a surpassing love for Mah\u00e2deva, and once he said of the Indian women\nof the future that if, amidst their new tasks, they would only remember now\nand then to say \"Shiva! Shiva!\" it would be worship enough. The very air of\nthe  Himalayas  was  charged  for  him with the image of that \"eternal\nmeditation\" that no thought of pleasure could break. And he understood, he\nsaid, for the first time this summer, the meaning of the nature-story that\nmade the Ganges fall on the head of the great God, and wander in and out\namongst His matted locks before She found an outlet on the plains below. He\nhad searched long, he said, for the words that the rivers and waterfalls\nuttered, amongst the mountains, before he had realized that it was the\neternal cry \"Bom! Bom! Hara! Hara!\"\n", "\"Yes!\" he said of Shiva one day, \"He is the great God \u2014 calm, beautiful, and\nsilent! And I am His great worshipper\".\n", "Again his subject was marriage, as the type of the soul's relation to God.\n\"This is why\", he exclaimed, \"though the love of a mother is in some ways\ngreater, yet the whole world takes the love of man and woman as the type. No\nother has such tremendous idealizing power. The beloved actually becomes\nwhat he is imagined to be. This love transforms its object\".\n", "Then the talk strayed to national types, and he spoke of the joy with which\nthe returning traveller greets once more the sight of the men and women of\nhis own country. The whole of life has been a subconscious education to\nenable one to understand in these every faintest ripple of expression in\nface and form.\n", "And again we passed a group of Sanny\u00e2sins going on foot, and he broke out\ninto fierce invective against asceticism as \"savagery\" . . . . But the sight\nof wayfarers doing slow miles on foot in the name of their ideals seemed to\nrouse in his mind a train of painful associations, and he grew impatient on\nbehalf of humanity at \"the torture of religion\". Then again the mood passed\nas suddenly as it had arisen and gave place to the equally strong statement\nof the conviction that were it not for this \"savagery\", luxury would have\nrobbed man of all his manliness.\n", "We stopped that evening at Uri Dak bungalow, and in the twilight we all\nwalked in the meadows and the bazaar. How beautiful the place was! A little\nmud fortress \u2014 exactly of the European feudal pattern \u2014 overhung the footway\nas it swept into a great open theatre of field and hill. Along the road\nabove the river lay the bazaar, and we returned to the bungalow by a path\nacross the fields, past cottages in whose gardens the roses were in bloom.\nAs  we  came, too, it would happen that here and there some child more\nventuresome than others would play with us.\n", "JUNE 20.\n", "The next day, driving through the most beautiful part of the pass and seeing\ncathedral rocks and an old ruined temple of the sun, we reached Baramulla.\nThe legend is that the Vale of Kashmir was once a lake and that at this\npoint the Divine Boar pierced the mountains with his tusks and let the\nJhelum go free. Another piece of geography in the form of myth. Or is it\nalso prehistoric history?\n"]
