["\n", "SOME CUSTOMS OF THE HINDUS\n(Brooklyn Standard Union, April 8, 1895)\n", "\nA special meeting of the Brooklyn Ethical Association with an address by\nSwami Vivekananda, the Hindu monk as the main feature, was held at the Pouch\nGallery, of Clinton avenue, last night. \"Some customs of the Hindus what\nthey mean, and how they are misinterpreted,\" was the subject treated. A\nlarge throng of people filled the spacious gallery.\n", "\nDressed in his Oriental costume, his eyes bright, and a flush mantling his\nface, Swami Vivekananda started to tell of his people, of his country, and\nits customs. He desired only that justice be shown to him and to his. In the\nbeginning of his discourse he said he would give a general idea of India. He\nsaid it was not a country but a continent; that erroneous ideas had been\npromulgated by travellers who had never seen the country. He said that there\nwere nine separate languages spoken and over 100 different dialects. He\nspoke severely of those who wrote about his country, and said their brains\nwere addled by superstition, and that they had an idea that everyone outside\nof the pale of their own religion was a horrible blackguard. One of the\ncustoms that had often been misinterpreted was the brushing of the teeth by\nthe Hindus. They never put hair or skin in their mouths, but use a plant.\n\"Hence a man wrote,\" said the speaker, \"that the Hindus get up early in the\nmorning  and  swallow a plant.\" He said the [custom of widows throwing\nthemselves under the] car of juggernaut did not exist, never had, and that\nno one knew how such a story started.\n", "\nSwami Vivekananda's talk on caste was most comprehensive and interesting. He\nsaid it was not a granted [graded] system of classes, but that each caste\nthought itself to be superior to all the others. He said it was a trade\nguild and not a religious institution. He said that it had been in existence\nfrom time immemorial, and explained how at first only certain rights were\nhereditary, but how afterward the ties were bound closer, and intermarriage\nand eating and drinking were restricted to each caste.\n", "\nThe speaker told of the effect that the mere presence of a Christian or\nMohammedan would have on a Hindu household. He said that it was veritable\npollution for a white man to step into a Hindu's presence, and that after\nreceiving one outside of his religion, the Hindu always took a bath.\n", "\nThe Hindu monk abused [?] the order of the Pariahs roundly, saying they did\nall the menial work, ate carrion and were the scavengers. He also said that\nthe  people who wrote books on India came only into contact with these\npeople, and not with genuine Hindus. He described the trial of one who broke\nthe rules of caste, and said that the only punishment inflicted was the\nrefusal of the particular caste to intermarry or drink or eat with him or\nhis children. All other ideas were erroneous.\n", "\nIn explaining the defects of caste, the speaker said that in preventing\ncompetition it produced stagnation, and completely blocked the progress of\nthe  people.  He  said that in taking away brutality it stopped social\nimprovements. In checking competition it increased population. In its favor,\nhe  said,  were  the  facts that it was the only ideal of equality and\nfraternity. That money had nothing to do with social standing in the caste.\nAll were equal. He said that the fault of all the great reformers was that\nthey thought caste was due only to religious representation, instead of\nascribing it to the right source, namely, the curious social conditions. He\nspoke  very bitterly of the attempts of the English and Mohammedans to\ncivilize the country by the bayonet and fire and sword. He said that to\nabolish caste one must change the social conditions completely ant destroy\nthe entire economic system of the country. Better, he said, that the waves\nof  the  [Bay  of] Bengal flow and drown all rather than this. English\ncivilization was composed of the three \"B's\" \u2014 Bible, bayonet, and brandy.\n\"That is civilization, and it has been carried to such an extent that the\naverage income of a Hindu is 50 cents a month. Russia is outside, saying.\n'Let's civilize a little,' and England goes on and on.\"\n", "\nThe monk grew excited as he walked up and down, talking rapidly about the\nway the Hindus had been treated. He scored the foreign educated Hindus, and\ndescribed their return to their native land, \"full of champagne and new\nideas\". He said that child-marriage was bad, because the West said so, and\nthat the mother-in-law could torture her daughter-in-law with impunity, as\nthe  son  could  not interfere. He said that the foreigners took every\nopportunity to abuse the heathen, because they had so many evils of their\nown that they wanted to cover them up. He said that each nation must work\nout its own salvation, and that no one else could solve its problems.\n", "\nIn speaking of India's benefactors he asked whether America had ever heard\nof David Herr [Hare], who established the first college for women, and who\nhad devoted so much of his life to education.\n", "\nThe  speaker  gave  a  number  of Indian proverbs that were not at all\ncomplimentary to the English. In closing he made an earnest appeal for his\nland. He said:\n", "\n\"It matters not as long as India is true to herself and to her religion. But\na blow has been struck at her heart by this awful godless West when she\nsends hypocrisy and atheism into her midst. Instead of sending bushels of\nabuses, carloads of vituperation and shiploads of condemnations, let an\nendless stream of love go forth. Let us all be men\"\n"]
