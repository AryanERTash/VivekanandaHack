["\n", "PERSONAL TRAITS\n", "(Critic, October 7, 1893)\n", "\n. . . It was an outgrowth of the Parliament of Religions, which opened our\neyes to the fact that the philosophy of the ancient creeds contains much\nbeauty  for  the moderns. When we had once clearly perceived this, our\ninterest in their exponents quickened, and with characteristic eagerness we\nset out in pursuit of knowledge. The most available means of obtaining it,\nafter the close of the Parliament, was through the addresses and lectures of\nSwami Vivekananda, who is still in this city [Chicago]. His original purpose\nin coming to this country was to interest Americans in the starting of new\nindustries among the Hindoos, but he has abandoned this for the present,\nbecause he finds that, as \"the Americans are the most charitable people in\nthe world,\" every man with a purpose comes here for assistance in carrying\nit out. When asked about the relative condition of the poor here and in\nIndia, he replied that our poor would be princes there, and that he had been\ntaken  through the worst quarter of the city only to find it, from the\nstandpoint of his knowledge, comfortable and even pleasant.\n", "\nA  Brahmin  of  the Brahmins, Vivekananda gave up his rank to join the\nbrotherhood of monks, where all pride of caste is voluntarily relinquished.\nAnd  yet  he  bears the mark of race upon his person. His culture, his\neloquence, and his fascinating personality have given us a new idea of\nHindoo civilization. He is an interesting figure, his fine, intelligent,\nmobile  face  in  its  setting of yellows, and his deep, musical voice\nprepossessing one at once in his favor. So it is not strange that he has\nbeen taken up by the literary clubs, has preached and lectured in churches,\nuntil the life of Buddha and the doctrines of his faith have grown familiar\nto us. He speaks without notes, presenting his facts and his conclusions\nwith the greatest art, the most convincing sincerity; and rising at times to\na rich, inspiring eloquence. As learned and cultivated, apparently, as the\nmost accomplished Jesuit, he has also something Jesuitical in the character\nof his mind; but though the little sarcasms thrown into his discourses are\nas keen as a rapier, they are so delicate as to be lost on many of his\nhearers. Nevertheless his courtesy is unfailing, for these thrusts are never\npointed so directly at our customs as to be rude. At present he contents\nhimself with enlightening us in regard to his religion and the words of its\nphilosophers.  He  looks forward to the time when we shall pass beyond\nidolatry \u2014 now necessary in his opinion to the ignorant classes \u2014 beyond\nworship, even, to a knowledge of the presence of God in nature, of the\ndivinity and responsibility of man. \"Work out your own salvation,\" he says\nwith  the  dying Buddha; \"I cannot help you. No man can help you. Help\nyourself.\"\n", "\n"]
