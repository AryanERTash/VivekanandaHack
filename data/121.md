["\n", "CHAPTER VII\n", "THE TRIANGLE OF LOVE\n", "\nWe may represent love as a triangle, each of the angles of which corresponds\nto one of its inseparable characteristics. There can be no triangle without\nall  its three angles; and there can be no true love without its three\nfollowing characteristics. The first angle of our triangle of love is that\nlove knows no bargaining. Wherever there is any seeking for something in\nreturn,  there  can,  be  no  real  love;  it becomes a mere matter of\nshop-keeping. As long as there is in us any idea of deriving this or that\nfavour from God in return for our respect and allegiance to Him, so long\nthere can be no true love growing in our hearts. Those who worship God\nbecause they wish Him to bestow favours on them are sure not to worship Him\nif those favours are not forthcoming. The Bhakta loves the Lord because He\nis lovable, there is no other motive originating or directing this divine\nemotion of the true devotee.\n", "\nWe have heard it said that a great king once went into a forest and there\nmet a sage. He talked with the sage a little and was very much pleased with\nhis  purity and wisdom. The king then wanted the sage to oblige him by\nreceiving a present from him. The sage refused to do so, saying, \"The fruits\nof the forest are enough food for me; the pure streams of water flowing down\nfrom the mountains give enough drink for me; the barks of the trees supply\nme with enough covering; and the caves of the mountains form my home. Why\nshould I take any present from you or from anybody?\" The king said, \"Just to\nbenefit me, sir, please take something from my hands and please come with me\nto the city and to my palace.\" After much persuasion, the sage at last\nconsented to do as the king desired and went with him to his palace. Before\noffering the gift to the sage, the king repeated his prayers, saying, \"Lord,\ngive  me  more children; Lord, give me more wealth; Lord, give me more\nterritory; Lord, keep my body in better health\", and so on. Before the king\nfinished saying his prayer, the sage had got up and walked away from the\nroom quietly. At this the king became perplexed and began to follow him,\ncrying aloud, \"Sir, you are going away, you have not received my gifts.\" The\nsage  turned  round to him and said, \"I do not beg of beggars. You are\nyourself nothing but a beggar, and how can you give me anything? I am no\nfool to think of taking anything from a beggar like you. Go away, do not\nfollow me.\"\n", "\nThere is well brought out the distinction between mere beggars and the real\nlovers of God. Begging is not the language of love. To worship God even for\nthe sake of salvation or any other rewards equally degenerate. Love knows no\nreward. Love is always for love's sake. The Bhakta loves because he cannot\nhelp loving. When you see a beautiful scenery and fall in love with it, you\ndo not demand anything in the way of favour from the scenery, nor does the\nscenery demand anything from you. Yet the vision thereof brings you to a\nblissful state of the mind; it tones down all the friction in your soul, it\nmakes you calm, almost raises you, for the time being, beyond your mortal\nnature and places you in a condition of quite divine ecstasy. This nature of\nreal love is the first angle of our triangle. Ask not anything in return for\nyour love; let your position be always that of the giver; give your love\nunto God, but do not ask anything in return even from Him.\n", "\nThe second angle of the triangle of love is that love knows no fear. Those\nthat love God through fear are the lowest of human beings, quite undeveloped\nas men. They worship God from fear of punishment. He is a great Being to\nthem, with a whip in one hand and the sceptre in the other; if they do not\nobey Him, they are afraid they will be whipped. It is a degradation to\nworship God through fear of punishment; such worship is, if worship at all,\nthe crudest form of the worship of love. So long as there is any fear in the\nheart, how can there be love also? Love conquers naturally all fear. Think\nof a young mother in the street and a dog barking at her; she is frightened\nand flies into nearest house. But suppose the next day she is in the street\nwith  her  child, and a lion springs upon the child. Where will be her\nposition now? Of course, in the very mouth of the lion, protecting her\nchild. Love conquers all fear. Fear comes from the selfish idea of cutting\none's self off from the universe. The smaller and the more selfish I make\nmyself, the more is my fear. If a man thinks he is a little nothing, fear\nwill  surely  come  upon him. And the less you think of yourself as an\ninsignificant person, the less fear there will be for you. So long as there\nis the least spark of fear in you there can be no love there. Love and fear\nare incompatible; God is never to be feared by those who love Him. The\ncommandment, \"Do not take the name of the Lord thy God in vain\", the true\nlover of God laughs at. How can there be any blasphemy in the religion of\nlove?  The  more you take the name of the Lord, the better for you, in\nwhatever way you may do it. You are only repeating His name because you love\nHim.\n", "\nThe third angle of the love-triangle is that love knows no rival, for in it\nis always embodied the lover's highest ideal. True love never comes until\nthe object of our love becomes to us our highest ideal. It may be that in\nmany cases human love is misdirected and misplaced, but to the person who\nloves, the thing he loves is always his own highest idea. One may see his\nideal  in  the vilest of beings, and another in the highest of beings;\nnevertheless, in every case it is the ideal alone that can be truly and\nintensely loved. The highest ideal of every man is called God. Ignorant or\nwise, saint or sinner, man or woman, educated or uneducated, cultivated or\nuncultivated, to every human being the highest ideal is God. The synthesis\nof all the highest ideals of beauty, of sublimity, and of power gives us the\ncompletest conception of the loving and lovable God.\n", "\nThese ideals exist in some shape or other in every mind naturally; they form\na part and parcel of all our minds. All the active manifestations of human\nnature are struggles of those ideals to become realised in practical life.\nAll the various movements that we see around us in society are caused by the\nvarious ideals in various souls trying to come out and become concretised;\nwhat  is  inside presses on to come outside. This perennially dominant\ninfluence of the ideal is the one force, the one motive power, that may be\nseen to be constantly working in the midst of mankind. It may be after\nhundreds of births, after struggling through thousands of years, that man\nfinds that it is vain to try to make the inner ideal mould completely the\nexternal conditions and square well with them; after realising this he no\nmore tries to project his own ideal on the outside world, but worships the\nideal itself as ideal from the highest standpoint of love. This ideally\nperfect ideal embraces all lower ideals. Every one admits the truth of the\nsaying that a lover sees Helen's beauty on an Ethiop's brow. The man who is\nstanding aside as a looker-on sees that love is here misplaced, but the\nlover sees his Helen all the same and does not see the Ethiop at all. Helen\nor Ethiop, the objects of our love are really the centres round which our\nideals become crystallised. What is it that the world commonly worships? Not\ncertainly this all-embracing, ideally perfect ideal of the supreme devotee\nand lover. That ideal which men and women commonly worship is what is in\nthemselves; every person projects his or her own ideal on the outside world\nand  kneels  before it. That is why we find that men who are cruel and\nblood-thirsty conceive of a bloodthirsty God, because they can only love\ntheir own highest ideal. That is why good men have a very high ideal of God,\nand their ideal is indeed so very different from that of others.\n"]
