["\n", "CHAPTER III\n", "MORNING TALKS AT ALMORA\n", "PLACE: Almora.\n", "TIME: May and June, 1898.\n", "The first morning the talk was that of the central ideals of civilization\n\u2014 in the West, truth; in the East, chastity. He justified Hindu marriage\ncustoms as springing from the pursuit of this ideal and from the woman's\nneed of protection, in combination. And he traced out the relation of the\nwhole subject to the philosophy of the Absolute.\n", "Another morning he began by observing that as there were four main castes\n\u2014 Brahmin, Kshatriya, B\u00e2niy\u00e2 [Vaishya], Shudra \u2014 so there were four great\nnational functions: the religious or priestly, fulfilled by the Hindus; the\nmilitary, by the Roman Empire; the mercantile, by England today; and the\ndemocratic,  by America in the future. And here he launched off into a\nglowing prophetic forecast of how America would yet solve the problems of\nthe Shudra \u2014 the problems of freedom and co-operation \u2014 and turned to relate\nto a non-American listener the generosity of the arrangements which that\npeople had attempted to make for their aborigines.\n", "Again it would be an eager r\u00e9sum\u00e9 of the history of India or of the Moguls,\nwhose greatness never wearied him. Every now and then throughout the summer\nhe would break out into descriptions of Delhi and Agra. Once he described\nthe Taj as \"a dimness, and again a dimness, and there \u2014 a grave!\"\n", "Another time he spoke of Shah Jehan, and then, with a burst of enthusiasm:\n\"Ah! He was the glory of his line! A feeling for and discrimination of\nbeauty that are unparalleled in history. And an artist himself! I have seen\na manuscript illuminated by him which is one of the art treasures of India.\nWhat a genius!\"\n", "Oftener still, it was Akbar of whom he would tell, almost with tears in his\nvoice and a passion easier to understand, beside that undomed tomb, open to\nsun and wind \u2014 the grave of Secundra at Agra.\n", "But all the more universal forms of human feeling were open to the Master.\nIn one mood he talked of China as if she were the treasure-house of the\nworld, and told us of the thrill with which he saw inscriptions in old\nBengali (Kutil?) characters over the doors of Chinese temples.\n", "Few  things  could  be more eloquent of the vagueness of Western ideas\nregarding Oriental peoples than the fact that one of his listeners alleged\nuntruthfulness as a notorious quality of that race. . . . The Swami would\nhave none of it. Untruthfulness! Social rigidity! What were these, except\nvery, very relative terms? And as to untruthfulness in particular, could\ncommercial life or social life or any other form of co-operation go on for a\nday if men did not trust men? Untruthfulness as a necessity of etiquette?\nAnd how was that different from the Western idea? Is the Englishman always\nglad and always sorry at the proper place? But there is still a difference\nof degree? Perhaps \u2014 but only of degree!\n", "Or he might wander as far afield as Italy, that \"greatest of the countries\nof Europe \u2014 land of religion and of art; alike of imperial organization and\nof Mazzini; mother of ideas, of culture and of freedom!\n", "One day it was Shivaji and the Mahrattas and the year's wandering as a\nSanny\u00e2sin that won him home to Raigarh. \"And to this day\", said the Swami,\n\"authority in India dreads the Sannyasin, lest he conceals beneath his\nyellow garb another Shivaji\".\n", "Often the enquiry \"Who and what are the Aryans?\" absorbed his attention; and\nholding that their origin was complex, he would tell us how in Switzerland\nhe had felt himself to be in China, so like were the types. He believed too\nthat the same was true of some parts of Norway. Then there were scraps of\ninformation about countries and physiognomies, an impassioned tale of the\nHungarian  scholar  who  traced  the Huns to Tibet, and lies buried in\nDarjeeling and so on. . . .\n", "Sometimes  the  Swami  would  deal  with the rift between Brahmins and\nKshatriyas, painting the whole history of India as a struggle between the\ntwo  and  showing  that  the  latter  had  always embodied the rising,\nfetter-destroying impulses of the nation. He could give excellent reason too\nfor  the  faith  that  was  in him that the K\u00e2yasthas of modern Bengal\nrepresented the pre-Mauryan Kshatriyas. He would portray the two opposing\ntypes  of culture: the one, classical, intensive and saturated with an\never-deepening sense of tradition and custom; the other, defiant, impulsive\nand liberal in its outlook. It was part of a deep-lying law of the historic\ndevelopment that R\u00e2ma, Krishna and Buddha had all arisen in the kingly, and\nnot in the priestly caste. And in this paradoxical moment Buddhism was\nreduced  to  a  caste-smashing  formula  \u2014 \"a religion invented by the\nKshatriyas\" as a crushing rejoinder to Brahminism!\n", "That was a great hour indeed when he spoke of Buddha; for, catching a word\nthat  seemed  to  identify  him  with  its anti\u2014Brahminical spirit, an\nuncomprehending listener said, \"Why, Swami, I did not know that you were a\nBuddhist!\n", "\"Madam\", he said, rounding on her, his whole face aglow with the inspiration\nof that name, \"I am the servant of the servants of the servants of Buddha.\nWho was there ever like him? \u2014 the Lord \u2014 who never performed one action for\nhimself \u2014 with a heart that embraced the whole world! So full of pity that\nhe \u2014 prince and monk \u2014 would give his life to save a little goat! So loving\nthat he sacrificed himself to the hunger of a tigress! \u2014 to the hospitality\nof a pariah and blessed him! And he came into my room when I was a boy and I\nfell at his feet! For I knew it was the Lord Himself!\n", "Many  times he spoke of Buddha in this fashion, sometimes at Belur and\nsometimes  afterwards.  And once he told us the story of Amb\u00e2p\u00e2li, the\nbeautiful courtesan who feasted him. . . .\n", "National feeling did not have it all its own way. For one morning when the\nchasm seemed to be widest, there was a long talk on Bhakti \u2014 that perfect\nidentity with the Beloved that the devotion of Ray Ramananda, the Bengali\nnobleman, before Chaitanya so beautifully illustrates:\n", "Four eyes met. There were changes in two souls.\nAnd now I cannot remember whether he is a man\nAnd I a woman, or he a woman and I a man!\nAll I know is, there were two, Love came, and\n", "there is one!\n", "It was that same morning that he talked of the Babists of Persia, in their\nera of martyrdom \u2014 of the woman who inspired and the man who worshipped and\nworked. And doubtless then he expatiated on that theory of his \u2014 somewhat\nquaint and surprising to unaccustomed minds, not so much for the matter of\nthe statement as for the explicitness of the expression \u2014 of the greatness\nand goodness of the young, who can love without seeking personal expression\nfor their love, and their high potentiality.\n", "Another day coming at sunrise when the snows could be seen, dawn-lighted,\nfrom the garden, it was Shiva and Um\u00e2 on whom he dwelt \u2014 and that was Shiva\nup there, the white snow-peaks, and the light that fell upon Him was the\nMother of the World! For a thought on which at this time he was dwelling\nmuch was that God is the Universe \u2014 not within it or outside it and not the\nuniverse God or the image of God, but He it, and the All.\n", "Sometimes all through the summer he would sit for hours telling us stories,\nthose cradle-tales of Hinduism whose function is not at all that of our\nnursery  fictions,  but much more like the man-making myths of the old\nHellenic world. Best of all these I thought was the story of Shuka, and we\nlooked on the Shiva-mountains and the bleak scenery of Almora the evening we\nheard it for the first time. . . .\n", "Shuka was indeed the Swami's saint. He was the type, to him, of that highest\nrealization to which life and the world are merely play. Long after, we\nlearned how Shri Ramakrishna had spoken of him in his boyhood as \"my Shuka\".\nAnd never can I forget the look, as of one gazing far into depths of joy,\nwith which he once stood and quoted the words of Shiva in praise of the deep\nspiritual significance of the Bhagavad-Git\u00e2 and of the greatness of Shuka:\n\"I know the real meaning of the teachings of the Bhagavad-Gita, and Shuka\nknows, and perhaps Vy\u00e2sa knows \u2014 a little!\"\n", "Another day in Almora the Swami talked of the great humanizing lives that\nhad arisen in Bengal, at the long inrolling wash of the first wave of modern\nconsciousness on the ancient shores of Hindu culture. Of Ram Mohan Roy we\nhad already heard from him at Naini Tal. And now of the Pundit Vidy\u00e2s\u00e2gar he\nexclaimed, \"There is not a man of my age in northern India on whom his\nshadow has not fallen!\" It was a great joy to him to remember that these men\nand Shri Ramakrishna had all been born within a few miles of each other.\n", "The Swami introduced Vidyasagar to us now as \"the hero of widow remarriage\nand of the abolition of polygamy\". But his favourite story about him was of\nthat day when he went home from the Legislative Council, pondering over the\nquestion  of  whether or not to adopt English dress on such occasions.\nSuddenly someone came up to a fat Mogul who was proceeding homewards in\nleisurely and pompous fashion in front of him, with the news \"Sir, your\nhouse  is  on fire!\" The Mogul went neither faster nor slower for this\ninformation, and presently the messenger contrived to express a discreet\nastonishment, whereupon his master turned on him angrily. \"Wretch!\" he said.\n\"Am I to abandon the gait of my ancestors because a few sticks happen to be\nburning?\" And Vidyasagar, walking behind, determined to stick to the Ch\u00e2dar,\nDhoti and sandals, not even adopting coat and slippers.\n", "The picture of Vidyasagar going into retreat for a month for the study of\nthe  Sh\u00e2stras,  when his mother had suggested to him the remarriage of\nchild-widows, was very forcible. \"He came out of his retirement of opinion\nthat they were not against such remarriage, and he obtained the signatures\nof the pundits that they agreed in this opinion. Then the action of certain\nnative princes led the pundits to abandon their own signatures so that, had\nthe government not determined to assist the movement, it could not have been\ncarried \u2014 and now\", added the Swami, \"the difficulty has an economic rather\nthan a social basis\".\n", "We could believe that a man who was able to discredit polygamy by moral\nforce alone, was \"intensely spiritual\". And it was wonderful indeed to\nrealize the Indian indifference to a formal creed when we heard how this\ngiant was driven by the famine of 1864 \u2014 when 140,000 people died of hunger\nand  disease \u2014 to have nothing more to do with God and become entirely\nagnostic in thought.\n", "With this man, as one of the educators of Bengal, the Swami coupled the name\nof David Hare, the old Scotsman and atheist to whom the clergy of Calcutta\nrefused  Christian burial. He had died of nursing an old pupil through\ncholera. So his own boys carried his dead body and buried it in a swamp and\nmade the grave a place of pilgrimage. That place has now become College\nSquare, the educational centre, and his school is now within the university.\nAnd to this day Calcutta students make pilgrimage to the tomb.\n", "On this day we took advantage of the natural turn of the conversation to\ncross-question the Swami as to the possible influence that Christianity\nmight have exerted over himself. He was much amused to hear that such a\nstatement had been hazarded, and told us with much pride of his only contact\nwith missionary influences, in the person of his old Scotch master, Mr.\nHastie. This hot-headed old man lived on nothing and regarded his room as\nhis boys' home as much as his own. It was he who had first sent the Swami to\nShri Ramakrishna, and towards the end of his stay in India he used to say,\n\"Yes, my boy, you were right, you were right! \u2014 It is true that all is God!\"\n\"I am proud of him!\" cried the Swami. \"But I don't think you could say that\nhe had Christianized me much!\" . . .\n", "We  heard charming stories too on less serious subjects. There was the\nlodging-house in an American city, for instance, where he had had to cook\nhis  own food, and where he would meet in the course of operations \"an\nactress who ate roast turkey every day, and a husband and wife who lived by\nmaking ghosts\". And when the Swami remonstrated with the husband and tried\nto persuade him to give up deceiving people, saying, \"You ought not to do\nthis!\" the wife would come up behind and say eagerly, \"Yes, sir! That's just\nwhat I tell him; for he makes all the ghosts, and Mrs. Williams takes all\nthe money!\"\n", "He  told  us  also  of  a  young  engineer, an educated man, who, at a\nspiritualistic gathering, \"when the fat Mrs. Williams appeared from behind\nthe screen as his thin mother, exclaimed, 'Mother dear, how you have grown\nin the spirit-world!' \"\n", "\"At this\", said the Swami, \"my heart broke, for I thought there could be no\nhope for the man\". But never at a loss, he told the story of a Russian\npainter who was ordered to paint the picture of a peasant's dead father, the\nonly description given being, \"Man! He stood in front of\nit, greatly overcome, and said, \"Father! Father! But at\nthis the Hindu monk was genuinely astonished.\n", "In spite of such general interests, however, the inner strife grew high, and\nthe thought pressed on the mind of one of the older members of our party\nthat the Master himself needed service and peace. A word or two was spoken \u2014 little, but enough \u2014 and he, after\nmany hours, came back and told us that he longed for quiet and would go\nalone to the forests and find soothing.\n", "And then, looking up, he saw the young moon shining above us, and he said,\n\"The Mohammedans think much of the new moon. Let us also, with the new moon,\nbegin a new life!\" And he blessed his daughter with a great blessing so that\nshe, thinking that her old relationship was broken, nor dreaming that a new\nand deeper life was being given to it, knew only that the hour was strange\nand passing sweet. . . .\n", "MAY 25\n", "He went. It was Wednesday. And on Saturday he came back. He had been in the\nsilence of the forests ten hours each day, but on returning to his tent in\nthe evenings he had been surrounded with so much eager attendance as to\nbreak the mood, and he had fled. He had discovered in\nhimself the old-time Sannyasin, able to go barefoot and endure heat, cold\nand scanty fare, unspoilt by the West. . . .\n", "JUNE 2.\n", ". . . . . .\n", "And then, as we sat working on Friday morning the telegram came, a day late,\nthat said: \"Goodwin died last night at Ootacamund\". Our poor friend had, it\nappeared, been one of the first victims of what was to prove an epidemic of\ntyphoid fever. And it seemed that with his last breath he had spoken of the\nSwami and longed for his presence by his side.\n", "JUNE 5.\n", "On Sunday evening the Swami came home. Through our gate and over the terrace\nhis way brought him, and there we sat and talked with him a moment. He did\nnot know our news, but a great darkness hung over him already, and presently\nhe broke the silence to remind us of that saint who had called the cobra's\nbite \"messenger from the Beloved\", one whom he had loved second only to Shri\nRamakrishna himself. \"I have just\", he said, \"received a letter that says:\n'Pavhari Baba has completed all his sacrifices with the sacrifice of his own\nbody. He knew himself what he was doing.\"\n", "Very little was said after this, and the party of monks passed on. Not yet\nhad the other news been broken.\n", "JUNE 6.\n", "Next  morning  he  came early in a great mood. He had been up, he said\nafterwards, since four. And one went out to meet him and told him of Mr.\nGoodwin's death. The blow fell quietly. Some days later he refused to stay\nin the place where he had received it, and complained of the weakness that\nbrought the image of his most faithful disciple constantly into his mind. It\nwas no more manly, he protested, to be thus ridden by one's memory than to\nretain the characteristics of the fish or the dog. As for Krishna, he was\nthe most shadowy of all. A poet, a cowherd, a great ruler, a warrior and a\nsage had all perhaps been merged in one beautiful figure holding the Git\u00e2 in\nhis hand.\n", "But today Krishna was \"the most perfect of the Avat\u00e2ras\". Half an hour later\n\"Naren\", as he then was, came in and found them huddled together discussing\nthe dangers of the case. He listened to what they had been told and then,\nlooking down, saw at his feet the cup of gruel that had been partly taken by\nShri Ramakrishna and which must have contained in it the germs of the fatal\ndischarges of mucus and pus, as it came out in his baffled attempts to\nswallow the thing on account of the stricture of the food-passage in the\nthroat.
