## Original Dataset Text
["THE STORY OF THE BOY GOPALA\n", "\"O mother! I am so afraid to go to school through the woods alone; other\nboys have servants or somebody to bring them to school or take them home-why cannot I have someone to bring me home?\"-thus said Gop\u00e2la, a little\nBrahmin boy, to his mother one winter afternoon when he was getting ready\nfor school. The school hours were in the morning and afternoon. It was dark\nwhen the school closed in the afternoon, and the path lay through the woods.\n", "Gopala's mother was a widow. His father who had lived as a Brahmin should-never  caring  for  the  goods of the world, studying and teaching,\nworshipping and helping others to worship\u2014died when Gopala was a baby. And\nthe poor widow retired entirely from the concerns of the world-even from\nthat little she ever had-her soul given entirely to God, and waiting\npatiently with prayers, fasting, and discipline, for the great deliverer\ndeath, to meet in another life, him who was the eternal companion of her\njoys and sorrows, her partner in the good and evil of the beginningless\nchain of lives. She lived in her little cottage. A small rice-field her\nhusband received as sacred gift to learning brought her sufficient rice; and\nthe piece of land that surrounded her cottage, with its clumps of bamboos, a\nfew cocoanut palms, a few mangoes, and lichis, with the help of the kindly\nvillage folk, brought forth sufficient vegetables all the year round. For\nthe rest, she worked hard every day for hours at the spinning-wheel.\n", "She was up long before the rosy dawn touched the tufted heads of the palms,\nlong before the birds had begun to warble in their nests, and sitting on her\nbed-a mat on the ground covered with a blanket-repeated the sacred names\nof the holy women of the past, saluted the ancient sages, recited the sacred\nnames of N\u00e2r\u00e2yana the Refuge of mankind, of Shiva the merciful, of T\u00e2r\u00e2 the\nSaviour Mother; and above all, (she) prayed to Him whom her heart most\nloved, Krishna, who had taken the form of Gopala, a cowherd, to teach and\nsave mankind, and rejoiced that by one day she was nearer to him who had\ngone ahead, and with him nearer by a day to Him, the Cowherd.\n", "Before the light of the day, she had her bath in the neighbouring stream,\npraying that her mind might be made as clean by the mercy of Krishna, as her\nbody by the water. Then she put on her fresh-washed whiter cotton garment,\ncollected some flowers, rubbed a piece of sandalwood on a circular stone\nwith a little water to make a fragrant paste, gathered a few sweet-scented\nTulasi leaves, and retired into a little room in the cottage, kept apart for\nworship. In this room she kept her Baby Cowherd; on a small wooden throne\nunder a small silk canopy; on a small velvet cushion, almost covered with\nflowers, was placed a bronze image of Krishna as a baby. Her mother's heart\ncould only be satisfied by conceiving God as her baby. Many and many a time\nher learned husband had talked to her of Him who is preached in the Vedas,\nthe formless, the infinite, the impersonal. She listened with all attention,\nand the conclusion was always the same-what is written in the Vedas must\nbe true; but, oh! it was so immense, so far off, and she, only a weak,\nignorant woman; and then, it was also written: \"In whatsoever form one seeks\nMe, I reach him in that form, for all mankind are but following the paths I\nlaid down for them\"-and that was enough. She wanted to know no more. And\nthere she was-all of the devotion, of faith, of love her heart was capable\nof, was there in Krishna, the Baby Cowherd, and all that heart entwined\nround the visible Cowherd, this little bronze image. Then again she had\nheard: \"Serve Me as you would a being of flesh and blood, with love and\npurity, and I accept that all.\" So she served as she would a master, a\nbeloved teacher, above all, as she would serve the apple of her eye, her\nonly child, her son.\n", "So she bathed and dressed the image, burned incense before it, and for\noffering?-oh, she was so poor!-but with tears in her eyes she remembered\nher husband reading from the books: \"I accept with gladness even leaves and\nflowers, fruits and water, whatever is offered with love\", and she offered:\n\"Thou for whom the world of flowers bloom, accept my few common flowers.\nThou who feedest the universe, accept my poor offerings of fruits. I am\nweak, I am ignorant. I do not know how to approach Thee, how to worship\nThee, my God, my Cowherd, my child; let my worship be pure, my love for Thee\nselfless; and if there is any virtue in worship, let it be Thine, grant me\nonly love, love that never asks for anything-'never seeks for anything but\nlove'.\" Perchance the mendicant in his morning call was singing in the\nlittle yard:\n", "Thy knowledge, man! I value not,\nIt is thy love I fear;\nIt is thy love that shakes My throne,\nBrings God to human tear.\n\nFor love behold the Lord of all,\nThe formless, ever free,\nIs made to take the human form\nTo play and live with thee.\n\nWhat learning, they of Vrind\u00e2's groves,\nThe herdsmen, ever got?\nWhat science, girls that milked the kine?\nThey loved, and Me they bought.\n", "Then, in the Divine, the mother-heart found her earthly son Gopala (lit.\ncowherd), named after the Divine Cowherd. And the soul which would almost\nmechanically move among its earthly surroundings-which, as it were, was\nconstantly floating in a heavenly ether ready to drift away from contact of\nthings material found its earthly moorings in her child. It was the only\nthing left to her to pile all her earthly joys and love on. Were not her\nmovements, her thoughts, her pleasures, her very life for that little one\nthat bound her to life?\n", "For years she watched over the day-to-day unfolding of that baby life with\nall a mother's care; and now that he was old enough to go to school, how\nhard she worked for months to get the necessaries for the young scholar!\n", "The necessaries however were few. In a land where men contentedly pass their\nlives poring over books in the the light of a mud lamp, with an ounce of oil\nin which is a thin cotton wick-a rush mat being the only furniture about\nthem-the necessaries of a student are not many. Yet there were some, and\neven those cost many a day of hard work to the poor mother.\n", "How for days she toiled over her wheel to buy Gopala a new cotton Dhoti and\na piece of cotton Ch\u00e2dar, the under and upper coverings, the small mat in\nwhich Gopala was to put his bundle of palm leaves for writing and his reed\npens, and which he was to carry rolled up under his arm to be used as his\nseat at school-and the inkstand. And what joy to her it was, when on a day\nof good omen Gopal attempted to write his first letters, only a mother's\nheart, a poor mother's, can know!\n", "But today there is a dark shadow in her mind. Gopala is frightened to go\nalone  through  the wood. Never before had she felt her widowhood, her\nloneliness, her poverty so bitter. For a moment it was all dark, but she\nrecalled to her mind what she had heard of the eternal promise: \"Those that\ndepend on Me giving up all other thoughts, to them I Myself carry whatever\nis necessary.\" And she was one of the souls who could believe.\n", "So the mother wiped her tears and told her child that he need not fear. For\nin those woods lived another son of hers tending cattle, and also called\nGopala; and if he was ever afraid passing through them, he had only to call\non brother Gopala!\n", "The child was that mother's son, and he believed.\n", "That day, coming home from school through the wood, Gopala was frightened\nand called upon his brother Gopala, the cowherd: \"Brother cowherd, are you\nhere? Mother said you are, and I am to call on you: I am frightened being\nalone.\" And a voice came from behind the trees: \"Don't be afraid, little\nbrother, I am here; go home without fear.\"\n", "Thus every day the boy called, and the voice answered. The mother heard of\nit with wonder and love; and she instructed her child to ask the brother of\nthe wood to show himself the next time.\n", "The  next day the boy, when passing through the woods, called upon his\nbrother. The voice came as usual, but the boy asked the brother in the woods\nto show himself to him. The voice replied, \"I am busy today, brother, and\ncannot come.\" But the boy insisted, and out of the shade of the trees came\nthe Cowherd of the woods, a boy dressed in the garb of cowherds, with a\nlittle crown on his head in which were peacock's feathers, and the cowherd's\nflute in his hands.\n", "And  they  were so happy: they played together for hours in the woods,\nclimbing trees, gathering fruits and flowers-the widow's Gopala and the\nGopala of the woods, till it was almost late for school. Then the widow's\nGopala went to school with a reluctant heart, and nearly forgot all his\nlesson, his mind eager to return to the woods and play with his brother.\n", "Months passed this wise. The poor mother heard of it day by day and, in the\njoy of this Divine mercy, forgot her widowhood, her poverty, and blessed her\nmiseries a thousand times.\n", "Then there came some religious ceremonies which the teacher had to perform\nin honour of his ancestors. These village teachers, managing alone a number\nof boys and receiving no fixed fees from them, have to depend a great deal\nupon presents when the occasion requires them.\n", "Each pupil brought in his share, in goods or money. And Gopala, the orphan,\nthe widow's son!-the other boys smiled a smile of contempt on him when\nthey talked of the presents they were bringing.\n", "That  night Gopala's heart was heavy, and he asked his mother for some\npresent for the teacher, and the poor mother had nothing.\n", "But she determined to do what she had been doing all her life, to depend on\nthe Cowherd, and told her son to ask from his brother Gopala in the forests\nfor some present for the teacher.\n", "The next day, after Gopala had met the Cowherd boy in the woods as usual and\nafter they had some games together, Gopala told his brother of the forest\nthe grief that was in his mind and begged him to give him something to\npresent his teacher with.\n", "\"Brother Gopala,\" said the cowherd, \"I am only a cowherd you see, and have\nno money, but take this pot of cream as from a poor cowherd and present it\nto your teacher.\"\n", "Gopala, quite glad that he now had something to give his teacher, more so\nbecause it was a present from his brother in the forest, hastened to the\nhome of the teacher and stood with an eager heart behind a crowd of boys\nhanding  over  their presents to the teacher. Many and varied were the\npresents they had brought, and no one thought of looking even at the present\nof the orphan.\n", "The neglect was quite disheartening; tears stood in the eyes of Gopala, when\nby a sudden stroke of fortune the teacher happened to take notice of him. He\ntook the small pot of cream from Gopala's hand, and poured the cream into a\nbig vessel, when to his wonder the pot filled up again! Again he emptied the\ncontents into a bigger vessel, again it was full; and thus it went on, the\nsmall pot filling up quicker than he could empty it.\n", "Then amazement took hold of everyone; and the teacher took the poor orphan\nin his arms and inquired about the pot of cream.\n", "Gopala told his teacher all about his brother Cowherd in the forest, how he\nanswered his call, how he played with him, and how at last he gave him the\npot of cream.\n", "The teacher asked Gopala to take him to the woods and show him his brother\nof the woods, and Gopala was only too glad to take his teacher there.\n", "The boy called upon his brother to appear, but there was no voice even that\nday. He called again and again. No answer. And then the boy entreated his\nbrother in the forest to speak, else the teacher would think he was not\nspeaking the truth. Then came the voice as from a great distance:\n", "\"Gopala, thy mother's and thy love and faith brought Me to thee; but tell\nthy teacher, he will have to wait a long while yet.\"\n"]
