["\n", "PRACTICAL VEDANTA\n", "PART I\n", "(Delivered in London, 10th November 1896)\n", "\nI have been asked to say something about the practical position of the\nVedanta philosophy. As I have told you, theory is very good indeed, but how\nare we to carry it into practice? If it be absolutely impracticable, no\ntheory is of any value whatever, except as intellectual gymnastics. The\nVedanta, therefore, as a religion must be intensely practical. We must be\nable to carry it out in every part of our lives. And not only this, the\nfictitious differentiation between religion and the life of the world must\nvanish, for the Vedanta teaches oneness \u2014 one life throughout. The ideals of\nreligion must cover the whole field of life, they must enter into all our\nthoughts, and more and more into practice. I will enter gradually on the\npractical side as we proceed. But this series of lectures is intended to be\na basis, and so we must first apply ourselves to theories and understand how\nthey  are worked out, proceeding from forest caves to busy streets and\ncities; and one peculiar feature we find is that many of these thoughts have\nbeen the outcome, not of retirement into forests, but have emanated from\npersons whom we expect to lead the busiest lives \u2014 from ruling monarchs.\n", "\nShvetaketu was the son of \u00c2runi, a sage, most probably a recluse. He was\nbrought up in the forest, but he went to the city of the Panch\u00e2las and\nappeared at the court of the king, Prav\u00e2hana Jaivali. The king asked him,\n\"Do you know how beings depart hence at death?\" \"No, sir.\" \"Do you know how\nthey return hither?\" \"No, sir.\" \"Do you know the way of the fathers and the\nway of the gods?\" \"No, sir.\" Then the king asked other questions. Shvetaketu\ncould not answer them. So the king told him that he knew nothing. The boy\nwent back to his father, and the father admitted that he himself could not\nanswer these questions. It was not that he was unwilling to answer these\nquestions. It was not that he was unwilling to teach the boy, but he did not\nknow these things. So he went to the king and asked to be taught these\nsecrets. The king said that these things had been hitherto known only among\nkings; the priests never knew them. He, however, proceeded to teach him what\nhe  desired  to  know. In various Upanishads we find that this Vedanta\nphilosophy is not the outcome of meditation in the forests only, but that\nthe very best parts of it were thought out and expressed by brains which\nwere busiest in the everyday affairs of life. We cannot conceive any man\nbusier  than an absolute monarch, a man who is ruling over millions of\npeople, and yet, some of these rulers were deep thinkers.\n", "\nEverything goes to show that this philosophy must be very practical; and\nlater on, when we come to the Bhagavad-Gita \u2014 most of you, perhaps, have\nread it, it is the best commentary we have on the Vedanta philosophy \u2014\ncuriously enough the scene is laid on the battlefield, where Krishna teaches\nthis philosophy to Arjuna; and the doctrine which stands out luminously in\nevery page of the Gita is intense activity, but in the midst of it, eternal\ncalmness. This is the secret of work, to attain which is the goal of the\nVedanta.  Inactivity,  as  we understand it in the sense of passivity,\ncertainly cannot be the goal. Were it so, then the walls around us would be\nthe most intelligent; they are inactive. Clods of earth, stumps of trees,\nwould  be the greatest sages in the world; they are inactive. Nor does\ninactivity become activity when it is combined with passion. Real activity,\nwhich  is  the goal of Vedanta, is combined with eternal calmness, the\ncalmness  which  cannot be ruffled, the balance of mind which is never\ndisturbed, whatever happens. And we all know from our experience in life\nthat that is the best attitude for work.\n", "\nI have been asked many times how we can work if we do not have the passion\nwhich we generally feel for work. I also thought in that way years ago, but\nas I am growing older, getting more experience, I find it is not true. The\nless passion there is, the better we work. The calmer we are, the better for\nus,  and  the more the amount of work we can do. When we let loose our\nfeelings, we waste so much energy, shatter our nerves, disturb our minds,\nand accomplish very little work. The energy which ought to have gone out as\nwork is spent as mere feeling, which counts for nothing. It is only when the\nmind is very calm and collected that the whole of its energy is spent in\ndoing good work. And if you read the lives of the great workers which the\nworld has produced, you will find that they were wonderfully calm men.\nNothing, as it were, could throw them off their balance. That is why the man\nwho  becomes angry never does a great amount of work, and the man whom\nnothing can make angry accomplishes so much. The man who gives way to anger,\nor hatred, or any other passion, cannot work; he only breaks himself to\npieces, and does nothing practical. It is the calm, forgiving, equable,\nwell-balanced mind that does the greatest amount of work.\n", "\nThe Vedanta preaches the ideal; and the ideal, as we know, is always far\nahead  of the real, of the practical, as we may call it. There are two\ntendencies in human nature: one to harmonise the ideal with the life, and\nthe  other  to  elevate  the life to the ideal. It is a great thing to\nunderstand this, for the former tendency is the temptation of our lives. I\nthink that I can only do a certain class of work. Most of it, perhaps, is\nbad; most of it, perhaps, has a motive power of passion behind it, anger, or\ngreed, or selfishness. Now if any man comes to preach to me a certain ideal,\nthe  first  step  towards  which is to give up selfishness, to give up\nself-enjoyment, I think that is impractical. But when a man brings an ideal\nwhich can be reconciled with my selfishness, I am glad at once and jump at\nit. That is the ideal for me. As the word \"orthodox\" has been manipulated\ninto various forms, so has been the word \"practical\". \"My doxy is orthodoxy;\nyour doxy is heterodoxy.\" So with practicality. What I think is practical,\nis to me the only practicality in the world. If I am a shopkeeper, I think\nshopkeeping the only practical pursuit in the world. If I am a thief, I\nthink  stealing  is  the best means of being practical; others are not\npractical. You see how we all use this word practical for things we like and\ncan do. Therefore I will ask you to understand that Vedanta, though it is\nintensely practical, is always so in the sense of the ideal. It does not\npreach an impossible ideal, however high it be, and it is high enough for an\nideal. In one word, this ideal is that you are divine, \"Thou art That\". This\nis the essence of Vedanta; after all its ramifications and intellectual\ngymnastics, you know the human soul to be pure and omniscient, you see that\nsuch superstitions as birth and death would be entire nonsense when spoken\nof in connection with the soul. The soul was never born and will never die,\nand all these ideas that we are going to die and are afraid to die are mere\nsuperstitions. And all such ideas as that we can do this or cannot do that\nare superstitions. We can do everything. The Vedanta teaches men to have\nfaith in themselves first. As certain religions of the world say that a man\nwho does not believe in a Personal God outside of himself is an atheist, so\nthe Vedanta says, a man who does not believe in himself is an atheist. Not\nbelieving in the glory of our own soul is what the Vedanta calls atheism. To\nmany this is, no doubt, a terrible idea; and most of us think that this\nideal can never be reached; but the Vedanta insists that it can be realised\nby every one. There is neither man nor woman or child, nor difference of\nrace or sex, nor anything that stands as a bar to the realisation of the\nideal, because Vedanta shows that it is realised already, it is already\nthere.\n", "\nAll the powers in the universe are already ours. It is we who have put our\nhands  before  our eyes and cry that it is dark. Know that there is no\ndarkness around us. Take the hands away and there is the light which was\nfrom the beginning. Darkness never existed, weakness never existed. We who\nare fools cry that we are weak; we who are fools cry that we are impure.\nThus Vedanta not only insists that the ideal is practical, but that it has\nbeen so all the time; and this Ideal, this Reality, is our own nature.\nEverything else that you see is false, untrue. As soon as you say, \"I am a\nlittle mortal being,\" you are saying something which is not true, you are\ngiving the lie to yourselves, you are hypnotising yourselves into something\nvile and weak and wretched.\n", "\nThe Vedanta recognises no sin, it only recognises error. And the greatest\nerror, says the Vedanta, is to say that you are weak, that you are a sinner,\na miserable creature, and that you have no power and you cannot do this and\nthat. Every time you think in that way, you, as it were, rivet one more link\nin the chain that binds you down, you add one more layer of hypnotism on to\nyour own soul. Therefore, whosoever thinks he is weak is wrong, whosoever\nthinks he is impure is wrong, and is throwing a bad thought into the world.\nThis we must always bear in mind that in the Vedanta there is no attempt at\nreconciling the present life \u2014 the hypnotised life, this false life which we\nhave assumed \u2014 with the ideal; but this false life must go, and the real\nlife which is always existing must manifest itself, must shine out. No man\nbecomes purer and purer, it is a matter of greater manifestation. The veil\ndrops away, and the native purity of the soul begins to manifest itself.\nEverything is ours already \u2014 infinite purity, freedom, love, and power.\n", "\nThe Vedanta also says that not only can this be realised in the depths of\nforests or caves, but by men in all possible conditions of life. We have\nseen that the people who discovered these truths were neither living in\ncaves nor forests, nor following the ordinary vocations of life, but men\nwho, we have every reason to believe, led the busiest of lives, men who had\nto command armies, to sit on thrones, and look to the welfare of millions\n\u2014 and all these, in the days of absolute monarchy, and not as in these days\nwhen a king is to a great extent a mere figurehead. Yet they could find time\nto think out all these thoughts, to realise them, and to teach them to\nhumanity. How much more then should it be practical for us whose lives,\ncompared with theirs, are lives of leisure? That we cannot realise them is a\nshame to us, seeing that we are comparatively free all the time, having very\nlittle to do. My requirements are as nothing compared with those of an\nancient absolute monarch. My wants are as nothing compared with the demands\nof Arjuna on the battlefield of Kurukshetra, commanding a huge army; and yet\nhe could find time in the midst of the din and turmoil of battle to talk the\nhighest philosophy and to carry it into his life also. Surely we ought to be\nable to do as much in this life of ours \u2014 comparatively free, easy, and\ncomfortable. Most of us here have more time than we think we have, if we\nreally want to use it for good. With the amount of freedom we have we can\nattain to two hundred ideals in this life, if we will, but we must not\ndegrade the ideal to the actual. One of the most insinuating things comes to\nus in the shape of persons who apologise for our mistakes and teach us how\nto make special excuses for all our foolish wants and foolish desires; and\nwe think that their ideal is the only ideal we need have. But it is not so.\nThe Vedanta teaches no such thing. The actual should be reconciled to the\nideal, the present life should be made to coincide with life eternal.\n", "\nFor you must always remember that the one central ideal of Vedanta is this\noneness. There are no two in anything, no two lives, nor even two different\nkinds  of life for the two worlds. You will find the Vedas speaking of\nheavens and things like that at first; but later on, when they come to the\nhighest ideals of their philosophy, they brush away all these things. There\nis but one life, one world, one existence. Everything is that One, the\ndifference is in degree and not in kind. The difference between our lives is\nnot in kind. The Vedanta entirely denies such ideas as that animals are\nseparate from men, and that they were made and created by God to be used for\nour food.\n", "\nSome people have been kind enough to start an antivivisection society. I\nasked a member, \"Why do you think, my friend, that it is quite lawful to\nkill  animals  for  food,  and  not  to kill one or two for scientific\nexperiments?\" He replied, \"Vivisection is most horrible, but animals have\nbeen given to us for food.\" Oneness includes all animals. If man's life is\nimmortal, so also is the animal's. The difference is only in degree and not\nin kind. The amoeba and I are the same, the difference is only in degree;\nand from the standpoint of the highest life, all these differences vanish. A\nman may see a great deal of difference between grass and a little tree, but\nif you mount very high, the grass and the biggest tree will appear much the\nsame. So, from the standpoint of the highest ideal, the lowest animal and\nthe highest man are the same. If you believe there is a God, the animals and\nthe highest creatures must be the same. A God who is partial to his children\ncalled men, and cruel to his children called brute beasts, is worse than a\ndemon. I would rather die a hundred times than worship such a God. My whole\nlife would be a fight with such a God But there is no difference, and those\nwho say there is, are irresponsible, heartless people who do not know. Here\nis a case of the word practical used in a wrong sense. I myself may not be a\nvery strict vegetarian, but I understand the ideal. When I eat meat I know\nit is wrong. Even if I am bound to eat it under certain circumstances, I\nknow it is cruel. I must not drag my ideal down to the actual and apologise\nfor my weak conduct in this way. The ideal is not to eat flesh, not to\ninjure any being, for all animals are my brothers. If you can think of them\nas your brothers, you have made a little headway towards the brotherhood of\nall souls, not to speak of the brotherhood of man! That is child's play. You\ngenerally find that this is not very acceptable to many, because it teaches\nthem to give up the actual, and go higher up to the ideal. To me\nthat is an impracticable religion. But if a man teaches a religion which\npresents the highest ideal, I am ready for him. If anyone wants to\npreach that way to us, poor, sense-bound clods of earth as we have made\nourselves by following that teaching, we shall never progress. We must not look\ndown with contempt on others. All of us are going towards the same goal. The\ndifference between weakness and strength is one of degree; the difference\nbetween virtue and vice is one of degree, the difference between heaven and\nhell is one of degree, the difference between life and death is one of\ndegree,  all differences in this world are of degree, and not of kind,\nbecause oneness is the secret of everything. All is One, which manifests\nItself, either as thought, or life, or soul, or body, and the difference is\nonly in degree. As such, we have no right to look down with contempt upon\nthose who are not developed exactly in the same degree as we are. Condemn\nnone; if you can stretch out a helping hand, do so. If you cannot, fold your\nhands, bless your brothers, and let them go their own way. Dragging down and\ncondemning is not the way to work. Criticism and condemnation is a\nvain way of spending our energies, for in the long run we come to learn that\nall are seeing the same thing, are more or less approaching the same ideal,\nand that most of our differences are merely differences of expression.\n", "\nTake the idea of sin. One shows\nto man his strength and the other his weakness. There may be weakness, says\nthe Vedanta, but never mind, we want to grow. Disease was found out as soon\nas man was born. Instead of telling them they are sinners, the Vedanta\ntakes the opposite position, and says, \"You are pure and perfect, and what\nyou  call  sin  does  not belong to you.\" Sins are very low degrees of\nSelf-manifestation; manifest your Self in a high degree. That is the one\nthing to remember; all of us can do that. Never say, \"No\", never say, \"I\ncannot\", for you are infinite. Even time and space are as nothing compared\nwith your nature. You can do anything and everything, you are almighty.\n", "\nThese are the principles of ethics, but we shall now come down lower and\nwork out the details. Religion, to help mankind, must be ready and able\nto help him in whatever condition he is, in servitude or in freedom, in the\ndepths of degradation or on the heights of purity; everywhere, equally, it\nshould be able to come to his aid. The old\nreligions said that he was an atheist who did not believe in God. The new\nreligion says that he is the atheist who does not believe in himself. Tell it\nto men who are weak and persist in telling it. You are the Pure One; awake\nand arise, O mighty one, this sleep does not become you. Awake and arise, it\ndoes not befit you. Think not that you are weak and miserable. Almighty,\narise and awake, and manifest your own nature. It is not fitting that you\nthink yourself a sinner. It is not fitting that you think yourself weak. Say\nthat to the world, say it to yourselves, and see what a practical result\ncomes,  see  how  with an electric flash everything is manifested, how\neverything is changed. Tell that to mankind, and show them their power. Then\nwe shall learn how to apply it in our daily lives.\n", "\nTo be able to use what we call Viveka (discrimination), to learn how in\nevery moment of our lives, in every one of our actions, to discriminate\nbetween what is right and wrong, true and false, we shall have to know the\ntest of truth, which is purity, oneness. It is a disintegrating power; it separates and destroys.\n", "\nLove binds, love makes for that oneness. The God of the Vedanta is the most known of all\nand is not the outcome of imagination.\n", "\nIf  this  is not preaching a practical God, how else could you teach a\npractical God?
