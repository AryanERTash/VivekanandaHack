["\n", "MEMOIRS OF EUROPEAN TRAVEL\n", "ADDENDA\n", "(These interesting jottings were found among Swamiji's papers \u2014 Ed.)\n", "The first view of Constantinople we had from the train. It is an ancient\ncity, with big drains running across the walls, narrow and crooked lanes\nfull of dirt, and wooden houses, etc., but in them there is a certain beauty\nowing to their novelty. At the station we had great trouble over our books.\nMademoiselle Calv\u00e9 and Jules Bois tried much, in French, to reason with the\noctroi officers, which gradually led to a quarrel between the parties. The\nhead of the officers was a Turk, and his dinner was ready; so the quarrel\nended without further complications. They returned all the books with the\nexception of two which they held back. They promised to send them to the\nhotel immediately, which they never did. We went round the town and bazar of\nStamboul  or  Constantinople.  Beyond the Pont or creek is the Pera or\nforeigners' quarters, hotels, etc., whence we got into a carriage, saw the\ntown, and then took some rest. In the evening we went to visit Woods Pasha,\nand the next day started on an excursion along the Bosphorus in a boat. It\nwas extremely cold and there was a strong wind. So I and Miss MacLeod got\ndown  at the first station. It was decided that we would cross over to\nScutari and see P\u00e9re Hyacinthe. Not knowing the language we engaged a boat\nby signs merely, crossed over, and hired a carriage. On the way we saw the\nseat of a Sufi Fakir. These Fakirs cure people's diseases, which they do in\nthe following manner. First they read a portion of their scriptures, moving\ntheir body backward and forward; then they begin to dance and gradually get\na sort of inspiration, after which they heal the disease by treading on the\npatient's body.\n", "We had a long talk with P\u00e8re Hyacinthe about the American Colleges, after\nwhich  we went to an Arab shop where we met a Turkish student. Then we\nreturned from Scutari. \u2014 We had found out a boat, but it failed to reach its\nexact destination. However, we took a tram from the place where we were\nlanded and returned to our quarters at the hotel at Stamboul. The Museum at\nStamboul is situated where the ancient harem of the Greek Emperors once\nstood.  We  saw some remarkable sarcophagi and other things, and had a\ncharming view of the city from above Topkhana. I enjoyed taking fried chick\npeas here after such a long time, and had spiced rice and some other dishes,\nprepared in the Turkish fashion. After visiting the cemetery of Scutari we\nwent to see the ancient walls. Within the walls was the prison \u2014 a dreadful\nplace. Next we met Woods Pasha and started for the Bosphorus. We had our\ndinner  with the French charg\u00e9 d'affaires and met a Greek Pasha and an\nAlbanian gentleman. The Police have prohibited P\u00e8re Hyacinthe's lectures; so\nI too cannot lecture. We saw Mr. Devanmall and Chobeji \u2014 a Gujar\u00e2ti Brahmin.\nThere are a good many Indians here \u2014 Hindustanis, Mussalmans, etc. We had a\ntalk on Turkish Philosophy and heard of Noor Bey, whose gradfather was a\nFrenchman. They say he is as handsome as a Kashmari. The women here have got\nno purdah system and are very free. Prostitution is chiefly a Mohammedan\npractice.  We  heard  of Kurd Pasha and the massacre of Armenians. The\nArmenians have really no country of their own, and those countries which\nthey  inhabit have generally a preponderating Mohammedan population. A\nparticular  tract  called  Armenia  is  unknown. The present Sultan is\nconstructing a Hamidian cavalry out of the Kurds who will be trained in the\nmanner of the Cossacks and they will be exempted from conscription.\n", "The Sultan called the Armenian and Greek Patriarchs and proposed to them\nconscription as an alternative for payment of taxes. They might thus serve\nto protect their motherland. They replied that if they went as soldiers to\nfight and died by the side of the Mohammedans, there would be some confusion\nabout the interment of Christian soldiers. The Sultan's rejoinder to this\nwas that it might be remedied by providing for both Mohammedan and Christian\npriests in each regiment, who would conduct the funeral service together\nwhen in the exigencies of battle the dead bodies of Christian and Mohammedan\nsoldiers would have to be buried in a heap all together, and there could\npossibly be no harm if the souls of men of one religion heard in addition\nthe  funeral  services  meant for those of the other religion. But the\nChristians did not agree \u2014 so they continue to pay taxes. The surest reason\nof their not acquiescing in the proposal was their fear lest by living with\nthe Mohammedans they might turn Mohammedan wholesale. The present Sultan of\nStamboul is a very hard-working man and he personally supervises everything,\nincluding  even  the  arrangement  of  amusements,  such as theatrical\nperformances etc., in the palace. His predecessor, Murad, was really a most\nunfit  man,  but the present Sultan is very intelligent. The amount of\nimprovement he has made in the condition of the State in which he found it\nat his accession is simply wonderful. The Parliamentary system will not be\nsuccessful in this country.\n", "At 10 in the morning we left Constantinople, passing a night and a day on\nthe sea, which was perfectly placid. By degrees we reached the Golden Horn\nand  the Sea of Marmora. In one of the islands of the Marmora we saw a\nmonastery of the Greek religion. Formerly there was ample opportunity for\nreligious education here, for it was situated between Asia on one side and\nEurope  on  the  other.  While  out  in  the morning on a visit of the\nMediterranean Archipelago we came across Professor Liper, whose acquaintance\nI had already made in the Pachiappa College at Madras. In one of the islands\nwe came upon the ruins of a temple, which had probably been dedicated to\nNeptune, judging from its position on the sea-shore. In the evening we\nreached Athens, and after passing a whole night under quarantine we obtained\npermission for landing in the morning. Port Peiraeus is a small town, but\nvery beautiful, having a European air about it in all respects, except that\none meets now and then with one or two Greeks dressed in gowns. From there\nwe drove five miles to have a look at the ancient walls of Athens which used\nto  connect the city with the port. Then we went through the town; the\nAcropolis, the hotels, houses, and streets, and all were very neat and\nclean.  The palace is a small one. The same day, again, we climbed the\nhillock and had a view of the Acropolis, the temple of the Wingless Victory,\nand the Parthenon, etc. The temple is made of white marble. Some standing\nremains of columns also we saw. The next day we again went to see these with\nMademoiselle Melcarvi, who explained to us various historical facts relating\nthereto. On the second day we visited the temple of Olympian Zeus, Theatre\nDionysius  etc., as far as the sea-shore. The third day we set out for\nEleusis, which was the chief religious seat of the Greeks. Here it was that\nthe famous Eleusinian Mysteries used to be played. The ancient theatre of\nthis place has been built anew by a rich Greek. The Olympian games too have\nbeen revived in the present times. They are held at a place near Sparta, the\nAmericans carrying off the palm in them in many respects. But the Greeks won\nin the race from that place to this theatre of Athens. This year they gave\nundisputed proof of this trait of theirs in a competition with the Turks\nalso. At 10 a.m. on the fourth day we got on board the Russian steamer,\nCzar, bound for Egypt. After reaching the dock we came to learn that the\nsteamer was to start at 4 a.m. \u2014 perhaps we were too early or there would be\nsome extra delay in loading the cargo. So, having no other alternative, we\nwent round and made a cursory acquaintance with the sculpture of Ageladas\nand his three pupils, Phidias, Myron, and Polycletus, who had flourished\nbetween 576 B.C. and 486 B.C. Even here we began to feel the great heat. In\na Russian ship the first class is over the screw, and the rest is only deck\n\u2014 full of passengers, and cattle, and sheep. Besides, no ice was available\nin this steamer.\n", "From a visit to the Louvre Museum in Paris I came to understand the three\nstages of Greek art. First, there was the Mycenoean art, then Greek art\nproper.  The Achaean kingdom had spread its sway over the neighbouring\nislands and also mastered all the arts that flourished there, being imported\nfrom  Asia. Thus did art first make its appearance in Greece. From the\nprehistoric times up to 776 B.C. was the age of the Mycenoean art. This art\nprincipally engaged itself in merely copying Asiatic art. Then from 776 B.C.\nto 146 B.C. was the age of Hellenic or true Greek art. After the destruction\nof the Achaean Empire by the Dorian race, the Greeks living on the continent\nand in the Archipelago founded many colonies in Asia. This led to a close\nconflict between them and Babylon and Egypt, which first gave rise to Greek\nart. This art in course of time gave up its Asiatic tinge and applied itself\nto an exact imitation of nature. The difference between Greek art and the\nart  of  other  countries consists in this, that the former faithfully\ndelineates the living phenomena of natural life.\n", "From 776 B.C. to 475 B.C. is the age of Archaic Greek art. The figures are\nyet stiff \u2014 not lifelike. The lips are slightly parted, as if always in\nsmiles. In this respect they resemble the works of Egyptian artists. All the\nstatues stand erect on their legs \u2014 quite stiff. The hair and beard etc. and\nall carved in regular lines and the clothes in the statues are all wrapped\nclose round the body, in a jumble \u2014 not like flowing dress.\n", "Next to Archaic Greek art comes the age of Classic Greek art \u2014 from 475 B.C.\nto 323 B.C., that is to say, from the hegemony of Athens up to the death of\nAlexander the Great. Peloponnesus and Attica were the states where the art\nof this period flourished most. Athens was the chief city of Attica. A\nlearned French art critic has written, \"(Classic) Greek art at its highest\ndevelopment freed itself completely from the fetters of all established\ncanons and became independent. It then recognised the art regulations of no\ncountry, nor guided itself according to them. The more we study the fifth\ncentury B.C., so brilliant in its art development \u2014 during which period all\nthe perfect specimens of sculpture were turned out \u2014 the more is the idea\nbrought home to our mind that Greek art owed its life and vigour to its\ncutting loose from the pale of stereotyped rules\". This Classic Greek art\nhad two schools \u2014 first, the Attic, and second, the Peloponnesian. In the\nAttic school, again, there were two different types \u2014 the first was the\noutcome  of the genius of the gifted sculptor, Phidias, which a French\nscholar has described in the following terms: \"A marvel of perfection in\nbeauty and a glorious specimen of pure and sublime ideas, which will never\nlose their hold upon the human mind\". The masters in the second type of the\nAttic school were Scopas and Praxiteles. The work of this school was to\ncompletely  divorce  art  from  religion and keep it restricted to the\ndelineation of merely human life.\n", "The chief exponents of the second or Peloponnesian school of Classic Greek\nart were Polycletus and Lysippus. One of these was born in the fifth century\nB.C., and the other in the fourth century B.C. They chiefly aimed at laying\ndown the rule that the proportion of the human body must be faithfully\nreproduced in art.\n", "From 323 B.C. to 146 B.C., that is, from the death of Alexander to the\nconquest of Attica by the Romans, is the period of decadence in Greek art.\nOne notices in the Greek art of this period an undue attention to gorgeous\nembellishments, and an attempt to make the statues unusually large in bulk.\nThen at the time of the Roman occupation of Greece, Greek art contented\nitself merely by copying the works of previous artists of that country; and\nthe only novelty there was, consisted in reproducing exactly the face of\nsome particular individual.\n"]
