["\n", "CHAPTER VI\n", "THE VALE OF KASHMIR\n", "PERSONS: The Swami Vivekananda and a party of Europeans and disciples,\namongst whom were Dhira Mata, the \"Steady Mother\"; one whose name was Jaya;\nand Nivedita.\n", "PLACE: The River Jhelum \u2014 Baramulla to Srinagar.\n", "TIME: June 20 to June 22.\n", "\"It  is  said  that  the Lord Himself is the weight on the side of the\nfortunate!\" cried the Swami in high glee, returning to our room at the Dak\nbungalow and sitting down with his umbrella on his knees. As he had brought\nno companion, he had himself to perform all the ordinary little masculine\noffices, and he had gone out to hire Dungas [houseboats] and do what was\nnecessary. But he had immediately fallen in with a man who, on hearing his\nname,  had  undertaken  the  whole  business and sent him back free of\nresponsibility.\n", "So we enjoyed the day. We drank Kashmiri tea out of a Samovar and ate the\njam of the country, and at about four o'clock we entered into possession of\na flotilla of Dungas, three in number, on which presently we set forth for\nSrinagar. The first evening, however, we were moored by the garden of the\nSwami's friend. . . .\n", "We found ourselves next day in the midst of a beautiful valley ringed round\nwith snow mountains. This is known as the Vale of Kashmir, but it might be\nmore accurately described, perhaps, as the Vale of Srinagar. . . .\n", "That first morning, taking a long walk across the fields, we came upon an\nimmense chennaar tree standing in the midst of a wide pasture. It really\nlooked as if the passage through it might shelter the proverbial twenty\ncows! The Swami fell to architectural visions of how it might be fitted up\nas a dwelling-place for a hermit. A small cottage might in fact have been\nbuilt in the hollow of this living tree. And then he talked of meditation,\nin a way to consecrate every chennaar we should ever see.\n", "We turned with him into the neighbouring farmyard. There we found, seated\nunder a tree, a singularly handsome elderly woman. She wore the crimson\ncoronet and white veil of the Kashmiri wife and sat spinning wool, while\nround her, helping her, were her two daughters-in-law and their children.\nThe Swami had called at this farm once before in the previous autumn and had\noften spoken since of the faith and pride of this very woman. He had begged\nfor water, which she had at once given him. Then, before going, he had asked\nher quietly, \"And what, Mother, is your religion?\" \"I thank God, sir!\" had\nrung out the old voice in pride and triumph. \"By the mercy of the Lord, I am\na Mussulman!\" The whole family received him now as an old friend and were\nready to show every courtesy to the friends he had brought.\n", "The journey to Srinagar took two to three days, and one evening, as we\nwalked in the fields before supper, one who had seen the Kalighat complained\nto the Master of the abandonment of feeling there, which had jarred on her.\n\"Why do they kiss the ground before the image?\" she exclaimed. The Swami had\nbeen  pointing  to the crop of Til \u2014 which he thought to have been the\noriginal of the English dill \u2014 and calling it \"the oldest oil-bearing seed\nof the Aryans\". But at this question he dropped the little blue flower from\nhis hands, and a great hush came over his voice as he stood still and said,\n\"Is it not the same thing to kiss the ground before that image as to kiss\nthe ground before these mountains?\"\n", "Our master had promised that before the end of the summer he would take us\ninto retreat and teach us to meditate. . . . It was decided that we should\nfirst see the country and afterwards make the retreat.\n", "The first evening in Srinagar we dined out with some Bengali officials, and\nin the course of conversation one of the Western guests maintained that the\nhistory of every nation illustrated and evolved certain ideals to which the\npeople of that nation should hold themselves true. It was very curious to\nsee  how the Hindus present objected to this. To them it was clearly a\nbondage  to which the mind of man could not permanently submit itself.\nIndeed, in their revolt against the fetters of the doctrine, they appeared\nto be unable to do justice to the idea itself. At last the Swami intervened.\n\"I think you must admit\", he said, \"that the ultimate unit is psychological.\nThis is much more permanent than the geographical\". And then he spoke of\ncases known to us all, of one of whom he always thought as the most typical\n\"Christian\" he had ever seen, yet she was a Bengali woman, and of another,\nborn in the West, who was \"a better Hindu than himself\". And was not this,\nafter  all, the ideal state of things, that each should be born in the\nother's country to spread the given ideal as far as it could be carried?\n"]
