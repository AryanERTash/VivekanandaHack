["\n", "BHAKTI\n(Delivered at Lahore on the 9th November, 1897)\n", "\nThere is a sound which comes to us like a distant echo in the midst of the\nroaring  torrents of the Upanishads, at times rising in proportion and\nvolume, and yet, throughout the literature of the Vedanta, its voice, though\nclear, is not very strong. The main duty of the Upanishads seems to be to\npresent before us the spirit and the aspect of the sublime, and yet behind\nthis wonderful sublimity there come to us here and there glimpses of poetry\nas we read;\n\u0928 \u0924\u0924\u094d\u0930 \u0938\u0941\u0930\u094d\u092f\u094b \u092d\u093e\u0924\u093f \u0928 \u091a\u0902\u0928\u094d\u0926\u094d\u0930\u0924\u093e\u0930\u0915\u0902 \u0928\u0947\u092e\u093e \u0935\u093f\u0926\u094d\u092f\u0941\u0924\u094b \u092d\u093e\u0928\u094d\u0924\u093f \u0915\u0941\u0924\u094b\u093d\u092f\u092e\u0917\u094d\u0928\u093f\u0903\n\u2014 \"There the sun shines not, nor the moon, nor the stars, what to speak of\nthis fire?\" As we listen to the heart-stirring poetry of these marvellous\nlines, we are taken, as it were, off from the world of the senses, off even\nfrom the world of intellect, and brought to that world which can never be\ncomprehended, and yet which is always with us. There is behind even this\nsublimity another ideal following as its shadow, one more acceptable to\nmankind, one more of daily use, one that has to enter into every part of\nhuman life, which assumes proportion and volume later on, and is stated in\nfull and determined language in the Pur\u00e2na, and that is the ideal of Bhakti.\nThe germs of Bhakti are there already; the germs are even in the Samhit\u00e2;\nthe germs a little more developed are in the Upanishads; but they are worked\nout in their details in the Puranas.\n", "\nTo understand Bhakti, therefore, we have got to understand these Puranas of\nours. There have been great discussions of late as to their authenticity.\nMany a passage of uncertain meaning has been taken up and criticised. In\nmany places it has been pointed out that the passages cannot stand the light\nof modern science and so forth. But, apart from all these discussions, apart\nfrom the scientific validity of the statements of the Puranas, apart from\ntheir  valid  or  invalid geography, apart from their valid or invalid\nastronomy, and so forth, what we find for a certainty, traced out bit by bit\nalmost  in  every  one  of  these volumes, is this doctrine of Bhakti,\nillustrated, reillustrated, stated and restated, in the lives of saints and\nin the lives of kings. It seems to have been the duty of the Puranas to\nstand as illustrations for that great ideal of the beautiful, the ideal of\nBhakti, and this, as I have stated, is so much nearer to the ordinary man.\nVery few indeed are there who can understand end appreciate, far less live\nand move, in the grandeur of the full blaze of the light of Vedanta, because\nthe first step for the pure Vedantist is to be Abhih, fearless. Weakness has\ngot  to  go  before a man dares to become a Vedantist, and we know how\ndifficult that is. Even those who have given up all connection with the\nworld, and have very few bandages to make them cowards, feel in the heart of\ntheir hearts how weak they are at moments, at times how soft they become,\nhow cowed down; much more so is it with men who have so many bandages, and\nhave to remain as slaves to so many hundred and thousand things, inside of\nthemselves and outside of themselves, men every moment of whose life is\ndragging-down slavery. To them the Puranas come with the most beautiful\nmessage of Bhakti.\n", "\nFor them the softness and the poetry are spread out, for them are told these\nwonderful  and marvellous stories of a Dhruva and a Prahl\u00e2da, and of a\nthousand saints, and these illustrations are to make it practical. Whether\nyou believe in the scientific accuracy of the Puranas or not, there is not\none among you whose life has not been influenced by the story of Prahlada,\nor that of Dhruva, or of any one of these great Paur\u00e2nika saints. We have\nnot only to acknowledge the power of the Puranas in our own day, but we\nought  to  be  grateful  to  them  as  they gave us in the past a more\ncomprehensive and a better popular religion than what the degraded later-day\nBuddhism was leading us to. This easy and smooth idea of Bhakti has been\nwritten and worked upon, and we have to embrace it in our everyday practical\nlife, for we shall see as we go on how the idea has been worked out until\nBhakti becomes the essence of love. So long as there shall be such a thing\nas personal and material love, one cannot go behind the teachings of the\nPuranas.  So long as there shall be the human weakness of leaning upon\nsomebody for support, these Puranas, in some form or other, must always\nexist. You can change their names; you can condemn those that are already\nexisting, but immediately you will be compelled to write another Purana. If\nthere arises amongst us a sage who will not want these old Puranas, we shall\nfind that his disciples, within twenty years of his death, will make of his\nlife another Purana. That will be all the difference.\n", "\nThis is a necessity of the nature of man; for them only are there no Puranas\nwho have gone beyond all human weakness and have become what is really\nwanted of a Paramahamsa, brave and bold souls, who have gone beyond the\nbandages of M\u00e2y\u00e2, the necessities even of nature \u2014 the triumphant, the\nconquerors, the gods of the world. The ordinary man cannot do without a\npersonal God to worship; if he does not worship a God in nature, he has to\nworship either a God in the shape of a wife, or a child, or a father, or a\nfriend, or a teacher, or somebody else; and the necessity is still more upon\nwomen than men. The vibration of light may be everywhere; it may be in dark\nplaces, since cats and other animals perceive it, but for us the vibration\nmust  be in our plane to become visible. We may talk, therefore, of an\nImpersonal Being and so forth, but so long as we are ordinary mortals, God\ncan be seen in man alone. Our conception of God and our worship of God are\nnaturally, therefore, human. \"This body, indeed, is the greatest temple of\nGod.\" So we find that men have been worshipped throughout the ages, and\nalthough  we  may condemn or criticise some of the extravagances which\nnaturally follow, we find at once that the heart is sound, that in spite of\nthese extravagances, in spite of this going into extremes, there is an\nessence, there is a true, firm core, a backbone, to the doctrine that is\npreached. I am not asking you to swallow without consideration any old\nstories, or any unscientific jargon. I am not calling upon you to believe in\nall sorts of V\u00e2m\u00e2ch\u00e2ri explanations that, unfortunately, have crept into\nsome of the Puranas, but what I mean is this, that there is an essence which\nought not to be lost, a reason for the existence of the Puranas, and that is\nthe teaching of Bhakti to make religion practical, to bring religion from\nits high philosophical flights into the everyday lives of our common human\nbeings.\n", "\n[The lecturer defended the use of material helps in Bhakti. Would to God man\ndid not stand where he is, but it is useless to fight against existing\nfacts; man is a material being now, however he may talk about spirituality\nand all that. Therefore the material man has to be taken in hand and slowly\nraised, until he becomes spiritual. In these days it is hard for 99 per cent\nof us to understand spirituality, much more so to talk about it. The motive\npowers  that are pushing us forward, and the efforts we are seeking to\nattain, are all material. We can only work, in the language of Herbert\nSpencer, in the line of least resistance, and the Puranas have the good and\ncommon sense to work in the line of least resistance; and the successes that\nhave been attained by the Puranas have been marvellous and unique. The ideal\nof Bhakti is of course spiritual, but the way lies through matter and we\ncannot help it. Everything that is conducive to the attainment of this\nspirituality in the material world, therefore, is to be taken hold of and\nbrought to the use of man to evolve the spiritual being. Having pointed out\nthat the Sh\u00e2stras start by giving the right to study the Vedas to everybody,\nwithout distinction of sex, caste, or creed, he claimed that if making a\nmaterial temple helps a man more to love God, welcome; if making an image of\nGod helps a man in attaining to this ideal of love, Lord bless him and give\nhim twenty such images if he pleases. If anything helps him to attain to\nthat ideal of spirituality welcome, so long as it is moral, because anything\nimmoral will not help, but will only retard. He traced the opposition to the\nuse of images in worship in India partly at least to Kabir, but on the other\nhand showed that India Has had great philosophers and founders of religions\nwho did not even believe in the existence of a Personal God and boldly\npreached that to the people, but yet did not condemn the use of images. At\nbest they only said it was not a very high form of worship, and there was\nnot one of the Puranas in which it was said that it was a very high form.\nHaving referred historically to the use of image-worship by the Jews, in\ntheir belief that Jehovah resided in a chest, he condemned the practice of\nabusing idol-worship merely because others said it was bad. Though an image\nor  any  other  material form could be used if it helped to make a man\nspiritual, yet there was no one book in our religion which did not very\nclearly state that it was the lowest form of worship, because it was worship\nthrough matter. The attempt that was made all over India to force this\nimage-worship on everybody, he had no language to condemn; what business had\nanybody to direct and dictate to anyone what he should worship and through\nwhat? How could any other man know through what he would grow, whether his\nspiritual growth would be by worshipping an image, by worshipping fire, or\nby worshipping even a pillar? That was to be guided and directed by our own\nGurus, and by the relation between the Guru and the Shishya. That explained\nthe rule which Bhakti books laid down for what was called the Ishta, that\nwas to say, that each man had to take up his own peculiar form of worship,\nhis  own way of going towards God, and that chosen ideal was his Ishta\nDevat\u00e2. He was to regard other forms of worship with sympathy, but at the\nsame time to practice his own form till he reached the goal and came to the\ncentre  where  no  more material helps were necessary for him. In this\nconnection a word of warning was necessary against a system prevalent in\nsome  parts  of India, what was called the Kula-Guru system, a sort of\nhereditary Guruism. We read in the books that \"He who knows the essence of\nthe Vedas, is sinless, and does not teach another for love of gold or love\nof anything else, whose mercy is without any cause, who gives as the spring\nwhich does not ask anything from the plants and trees, for it is its nature\nto do good, and brings them out once more into life, and buds, flowers, and\nleaves come out, who wants nothing, but whose whole life is only to do good\"\n\u2014 such a man could be a Guru and none else. There was another danger, for a\nGuru was not a teacher alone; that was a very small part of it. The Guru, as\nthe Hindus believed, transmitted spirituality to his disciples. To take a\ncommon material example, therefore, if a man were not inoculated with good\nvirus, he ran the risk of being inoculated with what was bad and vile, so\nthat by being taught by a bad Guru there was the risk of learning something\nevil. Therefore it was absolutely necessary that this idea of Kula-Guru\nshould vanish from India. Guruism must not be a trade; that must stop, it\nwas against the Shastras. No man ought to call himself a Guru and at the\nsame time help the present state of things under the Kula-Guru system.\n", "\nSpeaking of the question of food, the Swami pointed out that the present-day\ninsistence upon the strict regulations as to eating was to a great extent\nsuperficial, and missed the mark they were originally intended to cover. He\nparticularly instanced the idea that care should be exercised as to who was\nallowed to touch food, and pointed out that there was a deep psychological\nsignificance in this, but that in the everyday life of ordinary men it was a\ncare difficult or impossible to exercise. Here again the mistake was made of\ninsisting upon a general observance of an idea which was only possible to\none class, those who have entirely devoted their lives to spirituality,\nwhereas  the  vast majority of men were still unsatiated with material\npleasures, and until they were satiated to some extent it was useless to\nthink of forcing spirituality on them.\n", "\nThe highest form of worship that had been laid down by the Bhakta was the\nworship of man. Really, if there were to be any sort of worship, he would\nsuggest getting a poor man, or six, or twelve, as their circumstances would\npermit, every day to their homes, and serving them, thinking that they were\nN\u00e2r\u00e2yanas. He had seen charity in many countries and the reason it did not\nsucceed was that it was not done with a good spirit. \"Here, take this, and\ngo away\" \u2014 that was not charity, but the expression of the pride of the\nheart, to gain the applause of the world, that the world might know they\nwere becoming charitable. Hindus must know that, according to the Smritis,\nthe giver was lower than the receiver, for the receiver was for the time\nbeing God Himself. Therefore he would suggest such a form of worship as\ngetting  some  of these poor Narayanas, or blind Narayanas, and hungry\nNarayanas into every house every day, and giving them the worship they would\ngive to an image, feeding them and clothing them, and the next day doing the\nsame to others. He did not condemn any form of worship, but what he went to\nsay was that the highest form and the most necessary at present in India was\nthis form of Narayana worship.\n", "\nIn conclusion, he likened Bhakti to a triangle. The first angle was that\nlove  knew no want, the second that love knew no fear. Love for reward or\nservice of any kind was the beggar's religion, the shopkeeper's religion,\nwith  very little of real religion in it. Let them not become beggars,\nbecause, in the first place, beggary was the sign of atheism. \"Foolish\nindeed is the man who living on the banks of the Ganga digs a little well to\ndrink water.\" So is the man who begs of God material objects. The Bhakta\nshould be ready to stand up and say, \"I do not want anything from you, Lord,\nbut if you need anything from me I am ready to give.\" Love knew no fear. Had\nthey not seen a weak frail, little woman passing through a street, and if a\ndog barked, she flew off into the next house? The next day she was in the\nstreet, perhaps, with her child at her breast. And a lion attacked her.\nWhere was she then? In the mouth of the lion to save her child. Lastly, love\nwas unto love itself. The Bhakta at last comes to this, that love itself is\nGod and nothing else. Where should man go to prove the existence of God?\nLove was the most visible of all visible things. It was the force that was\nmoving the sun, the moon, and the stars, manifesting itself in men, women,\nand in animals, everywhere and in everything. It was expressed in material\nforces  as  gravitation  and  so on. It was everywhere, in every atom,\nmanifesting everywhere. It was that infinite love, the only motive power of\nthis universe, visible everywhere, and this was God Himself.[1]]\n", "\n"]
